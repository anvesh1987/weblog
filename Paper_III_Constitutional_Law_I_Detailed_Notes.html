Unit-I: Historical Background of the Indian Constitution

1. Constitution — Meaning and Significance 2. Evolution of Modern Constitutions 3. Classification of Constitutions 4. Indian Constitution — Historical Perspectives 5. Government of India Act, 1919 (Montague-Chelmsford Reforms) 6. Government of India Act, 1935 7. Framing of the Indian Constitution 8. Role of the Drafting Committee of the Constituent Assembly
1Constitution — Meaning and Significance

Meaning: A Constitution is the fundamental, organic law of a state that establishes the structure, powers, functions, and limits of government, and defines the relationship between the state and its citizens. It is the supreme law from which all other laws derive their validity.

Significance: 1. It creates and organizes the principal organs of government — the Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary — and distributes powers among them. 2. It defines the relationship between these organs, and between the government and the governed. 3. It serves as a check on arbitrary exercise of power by prescribing limits within which each organ must function (constitutionalism). 4. It embodies the fundamental values and aspirations of a nation (e.g., justice, liberty, equality, fraternity, as reflected in the Preamble to the Indian Constitution). 5. It provides mechanisms for its own amendment, allowing it to adapt to changing social and political needs while retaining stability.

2Evolution of Modern Constitutions

Modern constitutionalism traces its roots to several historical developments: 1. Magna Carta, 1215 (England): Often regarded as the first document to limit the powers of the sovereign and establish that even the king is subject to law. 2. English Bill of Rights, 1689: Established parliamentary supremacy over the crown and laid down certain civil and political rights. 3. American Constitution, 1787: The world’s first written constitution for a nation-state, establishing a federal structure, separation of powers, and a Bill of Rights (added later as the first ten amendments). 4. French Revolution, 1789 and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen: Popularized ideas of liberty, equality, fraternity, and popular sovereignty, profoundly influencing subsequent constitutional thought. 5. Post-World War Constitutions: The 20th century, particularly after the two World Wars and the decolonization movement, saw a proliferation of written constitutions in newly independent nations (including India), often incorporating both civil-political and socio-economic rights, reflecting welfare-state ideals alongside traditional liberal constitutionalism.

3Classification of Constitutions

Constitutions may be classified on various bases:

  1. Written and Unwritten: A written constitution is codified in a single (or a few) formal document(s) (e.g., India, USA); an unwritten constitution is not codified in one document but is derived from statutes, judicial precedents, and conventions accumulated over time (e.g., the UK).
  2. Rigid and Flexible: A rigid constitution requires a special, more difficult procedure for amendment than ordinary legislation (e.g., USA, and India to a significant extent for certain provisions); a flexible constitution can be amended by the same procedure as ordinary law (e.g., the UK, where Parliament can alter constitutional conventions/statutes through ordinary legislative process).
  3. Federal and Unitary: A federal constitution divides powers between a central authority and constituent units (states/provinces), each operating within its own sphere (e.g., USA, India to a large extent); a unitary constitution vests all powers in a single central authority, with local units deriving their powers from and remaining subordinate to it (e.g., UK, France).
  4. Monarchical and Republican: Based on whether the head of state is a hereditary monarch or an elected/appointed head (India is a republic with an elected President as head of state).
  5. Presidential and Parliamentary: Based on the relationship between the executive and legislature — in a presidential system, the executive is independent of and not responsible to the legislature (e.g., USA); in a parliamentary system, the executive (Council of Ministers) is drawn from and collectively responsible to the legislature (e.g., UK, India).

India’s Position: The Indian Constitution is written, largely rigid (though with flexible elements for certain provisions amendable by simple majority), federal in form with unitary features (often described as “quasi-federal” or having a “federal structure with unitary bias”), republican, and follows a parliamentary system of government.

4Indian Constitution — Historical Perspectives

The evolution of constitutional governance in India can be traced through a series of British-enacted statutes and events prior to independence: 1. Regulating Act, 1773: The first step by the British Parliament to regulate the affairs of the East India Company, establishing the office of Governor-General of Bengal. 2. Pitt’s India Act, 1784: Established dual control by the Crown and the Company over Indian administration. 3. Charter Acts (1793-1853): Periodically renewed the Company’s charter, gradually increasing parliamentary control and introducing legislative councils. 4. Government of India Act, 1858: Transferred power from the East India Company directly to the British Crown following the Revolt of 1857, marking the beginning of direct Crown rule. 5. Indian Councils Acts (1861, 1892, 1909): Progressively introduced limited legislative participation for Indians; the 1909 Act (Morley-Minto Reforms) notably introduced separate electorates for Muslims, a decision with long-term communal implications. 6. This sets the stage for the 1919 and 1935 Acts (discussed as separate topics below), which most directly shaped the structure eventually adopted in the Constitution of India.

5Government of India Act, 1919 (Montague-Chelmsford Reforms)

Background: Named after the Secretary of State for India (Edwin Montagu) and the Viceroy (Lord Chelmsford), this Act was based on the Montagu-Chelmsford Report and aimed at gradually developing self-governing institutions.

Salient Features: 1. Introduction of Dyarchy in the Provinces: Provincial subjects were divided into “transferred” subjects (administered by ministers responsible to the legislature, e.g., education, health, local government) and “reserved” subjects (administered by the Governor and his Executive Council, not responsible to the legislature, e.g., law and order, finance) — this dual system of governance was termed “dyarchy.” 2. Bicameral Legislature at the Centre: Established a Council of State (upper house) and a Central Legislative Assembly (lower house) for the first time at the central level. 3. Extension of the Separate Electorate System: Extended communal representation beyond Muslims to Sikhs, Christians, and other communities. 4. Limited Franchise: Voting rights were extended but remained limited by property, tax, and educational qualifications. 5. Establishment of a Public Service Commission: Recommended and eventually led to the setting up of a Public Service Commission in India.

Significance: Though dyarchy proved largely unworkable in practice (due to the artificial division of subjects and lack of real ministerial responsibility), the 1919 Act was the first serious attempt to introduce a responsible government element at the provincial level in India, and its shortcomings directly informed the reforms proposed in the 1935 Act.

6Government of India Act, 1935

Significance: Often described as the “blueprint” of the Indian Constitution, as a substantial part of the current Constitution’s structure and administrative provisions were borrowed from this Act.

Salient Features: 1. Proposal for an All-India Federation: Envisaged a federation of British Indian provinces and princely states (though this part never came into actual operation, since an insufficient number of princely states acceded). 2. Abolition of Dyarchy at the Provincial Level and Introduction of Provincial Autonomy: Provinces were given separate legal identities, and ministers became responsible to the provincial legislature for all provincial subjects. 3. Introduction of Dyarchy at the Centre (in place of the provinces): Central subjects were similarly divided into reserved and transferred categories (though, again, this federal part of the Act was never actually implemented before independence). 4. Three Lists of Subjects: Division of legislative powers into a Federal List, a Provincial List, and a Concurrent List — a scheme directly carried forward into the Seventh Schedule of the Indian Constitution. 5. Establishment of a Federal Court: Created the Federal Court of India (precursor to the present Supreme Court) with original, appellate, and advisory jurisdiction. 6. Provision for a Reserve Bank of India: Provided for the separation of currency and credit control from the Government of India, leading to the establishment of the RBI. 7. Extension of Separate Electorates and Franchise: Further extended communal representation and expanded the franchise, though it remained far short of universal adult suffrage.

7Framing of the Indian Constitution

The Constituent Assembly: Formed pursuant to the Cabinet Mission Plan (1946), the Constituent Assembly first met on 9 December 1946. It was not directly elected by universal adult franchise but was constituted through indirect election by members of the Provincial Legislative Assemblies.

Key Facts: 1. Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected as the President of the Constituent Assembly. 2. The Assembly worked through a number of committees to draft various parts of the Constitution — e.g., the Union Powers Committee, the Provincial Constitution Committee, the Fundamental Rights Sub-Committee, and, most significantly, the Drafting Committee. 3. The Constituent Assembly took 2 years, 11 months, and 18 days to complete the drafting process. 4. The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 (celebrated as Constitution Day / “Samvidhan Diwas”) and came into force on 26 January 1950 (celebrated as Republic Day) — the latter date was deliberately chosen to commemorate the “Purna Swaraj” (complete independence) resolution passed by the Indian National Congress on 26 January 1930. 5. The original Constitution (as adopted) contained a Preamble, 395 Articles, and 8 Schedules, organized into 22 Parts (the numbers have since increased through subsequent amendments).

8Role of the Drafting Committee of the Constituent Assembly

Composition: The Drafting Committee was constituted on 29 August 1947, comprising seven members, with Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as its Chairman.

Function: The Drafting Committee was entrusted with the crucial task of scrutinizing the draft Constitution prepared based on the work of the various other committees, and preparing a comprehensive draft for consideration and debate by the full Constituent Assembly, ensuring legal precision, internal consistency, and coherent structuring of the document.

Significance of Dr. B.R. Ambedkar’s Role: Given his central role in piloting the draft through prolonged debates, responding to thousands of amendments, and shaping the final text, Dr. Ambedkar is widely regarded as the principal architect (or “Father”) of the Indian Constitution. The Drafting Committee’s work involved reconciling diverse and sometimes conflicting viewpoints within the Assembly, and its final draft — after extensive discussion, debate, and amendment by the Constituent Assembly as a whole — became the Constitution of India as adopted on 26 November 1949.

Unit-II: Nature, Preamble and Citizenship

1. Nature and Salient Features of the Indian Constitution 2. Preamble to the Indian Constitution 3. Union and Its Territory 4. Citizenship 5. General Principles Relating to Fundamental Rights (Article 13) 6. Definition of “State” (Article 12) 7. Doctrine of Judicial Review
1Nature and Salient Features of the Indian Constitution

Salient Features: 1. Lengthiest Written Constitution in the World: Owing to the inclusion of detailed administrative provisions, provisions applicable to individual states, and provisions borrowed from multiple sources. 2. Blend of Rigidity and Flexibility: Certain provisions (e.g., those affecting the federal structure) require a special amending procedure (ratification by half the states), while many other provisions can be amended by a simple special majority of Parliament. 3. Federal System with Unitary Bias: While power is distributed between the Union and the States, several features (e.g., a single Constitution for both the Union and States, appointment of Governors by the Centre, emergency provisions, all-India services, and residuary powers with the Union) tilt the balance towards the Centre, leading commentators to describe it variously as “quasi-federal,” “cooperative federalism,” or federal with a “unitary bias.” 4. Parliamentary Form of Government: Both at the Union and State levels, based on the principle of collective responsibility of the executive to the legislature (drawn largely from the British model, but adapted with a written constitution and codified fundamental rights). 5. Synthesis of Parliamentary Sovereignty and Judicial Supremacy: While Parliament can amend the Constitution, this power is subject to judicial review and the basic structure doctrine, representing a balance rather than an adoption of either pure Parliamentary sovereignty (as in the UK) or pure judicial supremacy (as sometimes attributed to the USA). 6. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles: A unique combination of justiciable Fundamental Rights (Part III) and non-justiciable Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV), aiming to secure both political and socio-economic democracy. 7. Single Citizenship: Unlike some other federations (e.g., the USA), India provides for a single, common citizenship for the whole country, irrespective of the state in which a person is born or resides. 8. Independent Judiciary: An integrated judicial system headed by the Supreme Court, with High Courts and subordinate courts, having the power of judicial review. 9. Universal Adult Franchise: Every citizen above the prescribed age (currently 18 years) has the right to vote, without discrimination based on caste, religion, sex, or property. 10. Secularism: The State maintains no official religion and treats all religions with equal respect, guaranteeing freedom of religion to all persons (explicitly added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, though considered implicit even earlier).

2Preamble to the Indian Constitution

Text/Content: The Preamble declares India to be a “SOVEREIGN, SOCIALIST, SECULAR, DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC” securing to all its citizens Justice (social, economic, and political), Liberty (of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship), Equality (of status and opportunity), and promoting among them all Fraternity, assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the nation.

Key Points: 1. The words “Socialist” and “Secular” were inserted by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976; the original Preamble (1949) did not contain these words, though the concepts were considered implicit in the Constitution’s scheme from the outset. 2. Legal Status: In Berubari Union case (1960), the Supreme Court initially held that the Preamble is not a part of the Constitution and cannot be enforced in a court of law. However, in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), the Supreme Court held that the Preamble is part of the Constitution and can be used as an aid to interpretation where the language of a provision is ambiguous, and further, that the Preamble embodies the fundamental values/basic features of the Constitution (relevant to the basic structure doctrine). 3. Amendability: The Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati also held that the Preamble can be amended under Article 368, but Parliament cannot amend the basic features reflected in it. 4. Source of Authority: The opening words “We, the People of India” indicate that the Constitution derives its authority from the sovereign will of the people, not from any external authority (like the British Crown or Parliament).

3Union and Its Territory

Articles 1 to 4 deal with the Union and its territory: 1. Article 1: Declares “India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States” — significantly, the term “Union” (rather than “Federation”) was deliberately used to indicate that the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement between independent states (unlike the USA), and that no state has the right to secede from the Union. 2. Article 2: Empowers Parliament to admit into the Union, or establish, new states on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit. 3. Article 3: Empowers Parliament to form new states, alter the areas, boundaries, or names of existing states — importantly, this can be done by a simple majority through ordinary legislative process (not the special amendment procedure), reflecting the flexible, unitary aspect of Indian federalism (Parliament can, subject to certain procedural safeguards like referring the bill to the affected State Legislature for its views, redraw the internal map of India without invoking Article 368). 4. Article 4: Provides that laws made under Articles 2 and 3 (for admission/establishment of new states or alteration of boundaries) are not to be considered as amendments to the Constitution under Article 368, even though they may involve changes to the First and Fourth Schedules.

4Citizenship

Constitutional Provisions (Articles 5-11): The Constitution provides for citizenship at its commencement (26 January 1950) through birth, descent, registration (for persons from Pakistan or elsewhere), and for certain classes of persons of Indian origin residing outside India; Article 11 empowers Parliament to regulate the right of citizenship by law thereafter, which it has done through the Citizenship Act, 1955.

Modes of Acquiring Citizenship under the Citizenship Act, 1955: 1. By Birth: Generally, a person born in India on or after specified dates, subject to conditions relating to the citizenship/status of parents (the criteria have been made progressively stricter through amendments). 2. By Descent: A person born outside India to Indian citizen parents (subject to registration requirements in certain cases). 3. By Registration: Certain categories of persons (e.g., persons of Indian origin, spouses of Indian citizens) may apply for citizenship by registration. 4. By Naturalisation: A foreigner may acquire Indian citizenship after fulfilling prescribed conditions of residence and application. 5. By Incorporation of Territory: Persons of a territory that becomes part of India may be specified as citizens by the Government of India.

Loss of Citizenship: By renunciation (voluntary act), termination (voluntary acquisition of citizenship of another country), or deprivation (by an order of the Government on specified grounds like fraud, disloyalty).

Single Citizenship: India follows the concept of a single citizenship for the entire country (unlike the USA’s dual citizenship of the nation and the individual state), meaning all citizens, regardless of the state they reside in, enjoy the same rights of citizenship uniformly throughout the territory of India.

5General Principles Relating to Fundamental Rights (Article 13)

Article 13 is the key provision giving teeth to the Fundamental Rights in Part III by declaring the consequences of inconsistency with them: 1. Article 13(1): All laws in force in India immediately before the commencement of the Constitution, insofar as they are inconsistent with the provisions of Part III, shall, to the extent of such inconsistency, be void. 2. Article 13(2): The State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges the rights conferred by Part III, and any law made in contravention of this clause shall, to the extent of the contravention, be void. 3. Doctrine of Eclipse: A pre-constitutional law inconsistent with Fundamental Rights is not wiped out entirely but becomes unenforceable (dormant/eclipsed) to the extent of the inconsistency; it can become operative again if the inconsistent Fundamental Right is subsequently amended in a way that removes the inconsistency — Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955). 4. Doctrine of Severability: Where only a part of a statute is unconstitutional, only that offending part is declared void, and the rest of the statute remains valid, provided the valid part can be separated from the invalid part without altering the nature/scheme of the legislation — A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) applied this principle. 5. Article 13(3): Defines “law” broadly to include ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications, and customs/usages having the force of law — ensuring the widest possible range of state action can be tested against Fundamental Rights. 6. Article 13(4) (added by the 24th Amendment, 1971): Clarifies that “law” under this Article does not include a constitutional amendment made under Article 368 — this provision was central to the debate in Kesavananda Bharati about whether constitutional amendments themselves could be challenged for violating Fundamental Rights (ultimately resolved through the basic structure doctrine rather than through Article 13 itself).

6Definition of “State” (Article 12)

Article 12 defines “the State” for the purposes of Part III (Fundamental Rights), so as to identify against whom Fundamental Rights can be enforced. “The State” includes: 1. The Government and Parliament of India; 2. The Government and Legislature of each of the States; 3. All local authorities (municipalities, panchayats, district boards, etc.) within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India; and 4. Other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.

The “Other Authorities” Test: The scope of “other authorities” has been the subject of extensive judicial interpretation, since many bodies (statutory corporations, government companies, universities, etc.) are not literally “government” but perform public functions. In Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib Sehravardi (1981), the Supreme Court laid down several tests to determine whether a body is an “instrumentality or agency of the State,” including: (a) whether the entire share capital is held by the government; (b) whether the body enjoys a monopoly status conferred by the state; (c) the extent of government control over its management and policies; (d) whether the functions performed are of public importance and closely related to governmental functions; and (e) whether a department of government has been transferred to the body. No single test is conclusive; courts examine the cumulative effect of all relevant factors.

7Doctrine of Judicial Review

Meaning: Judicial review is the power of courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders, and to declare them void if they are found to be inconsistent with the Constitution.

Constitutional Basis: Though the phrase “judicial review” does not appear expressly, it is implicit in several Articles — Article 13 (voiding laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights), Article 32 (right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights), Article 226 (power of High Courts to issue writs), and Article 137/141 (Supreme Court’s powers and the binding nature of its decisions).

Significance and Scope: Judicial review extends to (a) constitutional amendments (subject to the basic structure doctrine), (b) ordinary legislation (both Union and State laws), and (c) administrative/executive action. The Supreme Court, in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), held that judicial review itself is a part of the basic structure of the Constitution, and hence cannot be taken away even by a constitutional amendment — this makes judicial review one of the most powerful and entrenched features of Indian constitutionalism, distinguishing it from systems (like the traditional British model) based on unqualified parliamentary sovereignty.

Unit-III: Fundamental Rights I

1. Right to Equality (Article 14) 2. Prohibition of Discrimination and Equality of Opportunity (Articles 15-16) 3. Abolition of Untouchability and Titles (Articles 17-18) 4. Freedoms under Article 19 5. Reasonable Restrictions on Article 19 Freedoms 6. Protection under Article 20 7. Right to Life and Personal Liberty (Article 21) and Right to Education (Article 21A) 8. Protection against Arrest and Preventive Detention (Article 22)
1Right to Equality (Article 14)

Text: Article 14 guarantees that “The State shall not deny to any person equality before law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India.”

Two Concepts: 1. Equality before Law (a concept borrowed from British/Diceyan jurisprudence): A negative concept implying the absence of any special privilege in favour of any individual, and the equal subjection of all persons (including officials) to the ordinary law of the land. 2. Equal Protection of Laws (borrowed from the American Constitution): A positive concept implying that persons in similar circumstances should be treated alike, both in terms of privileges conferred and liabilities imposed.

Reasonable Classification Test: Article 14 permits the State to make reasonable classifications for the purpose of legislation, provided the classification satisfies two conditions (laid down in numerous cases, e.g., State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952)): 1. The classification must be founded on an intelligible differentia (a clear basis of distinction) which distinguishes persons/things grouped together from others left out of the group; and 2. The differentia must have a rational nexus (relation) to the object sought to be achieved by the statute.

New Doctrine of Non-Arbitrariness: In E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974) and subsequently affirmed in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), the Supreme Court expanded Article 14 beyond the classical classification test, holding that Article 14 strikes at arbitrariness in state action, since arbitrariness is antithetical to the very concept of equality — meaning a law/action can be struck down as violating Article 14 even without a strict classification analysis, if it is found to be manifestly arbitrary or unreasonable.

2Prohibition of Discrimination and Equality of Opportunity (Articles 15-16)

Article 15 — Prohibition of Discrimination: 1. Article 15(1): Prohibits the State from discriminating against any citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth, or any of them. 2. Article 15(2): Prohibits discrimination based on these grounds with regard to access to shops, public restaurants, hotels, places of public entertainment, and the use of wells, tanks, roads, etc., maintained wholly/partly out of State funds or dedicated to public use. 3. Article 15(3): Permits the State to make special provisions for women and children (e.g., reservation of seats for women in local bodies, maternity benefits), recognizing that formal equality may sometimes require differential treatment to achieve substantive equality. 4. Article 15(4) (added by the 1st Amendment, 1951, following State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan): Permits the State to make special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes, or for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. 5. Article 15(5) and 15(6): Later additions permitting reservations in educational institutions (including private, unaided institutions, with certain exceptions) and for economically weaker sections (EWS).

Article 16 — Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment: 1. Article 16(1) and (2): Guarantee equality of opportunity for all citizens in matters relating to employment/appointment to any office under the State, and prohibit discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth, or residence. 2. Article 16(4): Enables the State to make provision for reservation of appointments/posts in favour of any backward class of citizens which, in the opinion of the State, is not adequately represented in the services under the State. 3. Subsequent clauses (16(4A), 16(4B)) permit reservation in promotions and carry-forward of unfilled reserved vacancies for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, subject to conditions laid down by the Supreme Court (e.g., Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) — the “Mandal Commission case” — which upheld reservations for OBCs but capped total reservations generally at 50%, and excluded the “creamy layer” from OBC reservation benefits).

3Abolition of Untouchability and Titles (Articles 17-18)

Article 17 — Abolition of Untouchability: Declares “Untouchability” is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden; the enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability is an offence punishable in accordance with law. This is one of the few Fundamental Rights enforceable directly against private individuals as well as the State, given the article’s social reform objective, and is given statutory teeth through the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989.

Article 18 — Abolition of Titles: Prohibits the State from conferring any title (other than a military or academic distinction) on any person, whether a citizen or non-citizen. It also prohibits an Indian citizen from accepting any title from a foreign state, and prohibits any person holding an office of profit/trust under the State from accepting any present, emolument, or title from a foreign state without the consent of the President. The provision reflects the constitutional commitment to democratic equality by prohibiting the creation of a titled aristocracy (like the erstwhile British-era titles of “Rai Bahadur” or “Khan Bahadur”).

4Freedoms under Article 19

Article 19(1) originally guaranteed seven freedoms to citizens; one (right to property) was removed by the 44th Amendment (1978) and made a mere legal right under Article 300A, leaving six freedoms: 1. Freedom of speech and expression [19(1)(a)] 2. Freedom to assemble peaceably and without arms [19(1)(b)] 3. Freedom to form associations or unions (or cooperative societies) [19(1)(c)] 4. Freedom to move freely throughout the territory of India [19(1)(d)] 5. Freedom to reside and settle in any part of the territory of India [19(1)(e)] 6. Freedom to practise any profession, or to carry on any occupation, trade or business [19(1)(g)]

These freedoms are available only to citizens, not to foreigners or (generally) to corporations (though shareholders of a company may sometimes invoke these rights in relation to the company’s business, per case law like Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India (1972)).

5Reasonable Restrictions on Article 19 Freedoms

Each of the six freedoms under Article 19(1) is subject to “reasonable restrictions” that the State may impose by law, set out in Article 19(2) through 19(6) respectively — grounds vary by freedom but broadly include: sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence (for speech under 19(2)); and, for the freedom to carry on any profession/trade/business, the interests of the general public and the State’s power to prescribe professional/technical qualifications or carry on trade/business itself to the exclusion of citizens (19(6)).

Test of “Reasonableness”: Courts examine both the substantive reasonableness (whether the restriction itself is proportionate and not excessive) and the procedural reasonableness (whether a fair procedure was followed in imposing it) of any restriction. The restriction must have a direct and proximate nexus with one of the specified grounds; a restriction going beyond what is necessary to achieve that object may be struck down as unreasonable.

6Protection under Article 20

Article 20 provides certain protections in respect of conviction for offences, available to all persons (not just citizens), and significantly, these protections cannot be suspended even during a national emergency (per Article 359, discussed later): 1. Article 20(1) — Protection against Ex-Post Facto Laws: No person shall be convicted of any offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act, nor be subjected to a penalty greater than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force at the time of the commission of the offence. (Note: this protection applies only to criminal, not civil, retrospective legislation, and does not prevent a retrospective increase in a civil liability or tax.) 2. Article 20(2) — Protection against Double Jeopardy: No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once (embodying the maxim “nemo debet bis vexari pro una et eadem causa” — no person should be vexed twice for the same cause). This bars a second prosecution and punishment for the same offence only where there has already been a prosecution and a punishment in the first instance (mere prosecution without punishment, or departmental proceedings not amounting to a “prosecution” before a court, do not attract this bar). 3. Article 20(3) — Privilege against Self-Incrimination: No person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself. This protects an accused person from being forced to make a statement/produce evidence which might incriminate them, though it does not extend to compelling production of certain kinds of objective/physical evidence (like fingerprints, blood samples in some contexts) which do not amount to “testimonial compulsion” — Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010) extended this protection to bar involuntary administration of narco-analysis, polygraph, and brain-mapping tests on accused persons.

7Right to Life and Personal Liberty (Article 21) and Right to Education (Article 21A)

Article 21: “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.”

Evolution through Judicial Interpretation: 1. In A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950), the Supreme Court initially interpreted “procedure established by law” narrowly — as long as there was some procedure prescribed by a validly enacted law, Article 21 was satisfied, regardless of whether the procedure itself was fair or reasonable. 2. This narrow view was overturned in the landmark case of Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where the Supreme Court held that the “procedure established by law” under Article 21 must be fair, just, and reasonable, not arbitrary, fanciful, or oppressive — effectively infusing an element similar to the American “due process” doctrine into Article 21, and establishing that Articles 14, 19, and 21 are interlinked (the “golden triangle”) rather than mutually exclusive. 3. Following Maneka Gandhi, the scope of “life” under Article 21 has been progressively expanded by judicial interpretation to include the right to livelihood (Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation), right to a clean and healthy environment, right to privacy (K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) — a nine-judge bench unanimously held privacy to be a fundamental right intrinsic to Article 21), right to speedy trial, right to legal aid, right to shelter, right to health, and various other dimensions of dignified human existence.

Article 21A (added by the 86th Amendment, 2002): Provides that the State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of 6 to 14 years, in such manner as the State may, by law, determine — a Fundamental Right complementing the Directive Principle originally found in Article 45 (which has since been amended to focus on early childhood care for children below 6).

8Protection against Arrest and Preventive Detention (Article 22)

Article 22 provides procedural safeguards to persons who are arrested or detained, divided into two parts:

  1. Rights of a Person Arrested under Ordinary Law [Article 22(1) and (2)]:
    • Right to be informed, as soon as possible, of the grounds for the arrest.
    • Right to consult, and be defended by, a legal practitioner of their choice.
    • Right to be produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours of arrest (excluding travel time), and not to be detained beyond this period without the magistrate’s authority.
  2. Preventive Detention [Article 22(3) to (7)]: These clauses carve out an exception, providing that the above safeguards do not apply to (a) an enemy alien, or (b) a person arrested/detained under a law providing for preventive detention (detention without trial, to prevent a person from acting in a manner prejudicial to specified interests like state security or public order, rather than to punish them for an offence already committed).
    • However, even for preventive detention, the Constitution provides certain safeguards: a person cannot generally be detained for more than three months without obtaining the opinion of an Advisory Board; the grounds of detention must be communicated to the detained person as soon as possible (subject to the State withholding facts considered against the public interest to disclose); and the detained person must be given the earliest opportunity to make a representation against the detention order.
    • Parliament is empowered to enact laws providing for preventive detention for reasons connected with defence, foreign affairs, or the security of India (Union List), while both Parliament and State Legislatures can legislate on preventive detention for reasons connected with the security of a state, maintenance of public order, or maintenance of supplies/services essential to the community (Concurrent List). Preventive detention remains a controversial and much-criticized feature of Indian constitutional law, given the significant curtailment of the ordinary Article 22(1)-(2) safeguards that it involves.

Unit-IV: Fundamental Rights II

1. Rights against Exploitation (Articles 23-24) 2. Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25-28) 3. Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29-30) 4. Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32) and Writs 5. Saving of Certain Laws (Articles 31A, 31B, 31C) 6. Effect of Emergency on Fundamental Rights (Articles 358 and 359)
1Rights against Exploitation (Articles 23-24)

Article 23 — Prohibition of Traffic in Human Beings and Forced Labour: 1. Prohibits “traffic in human beings,” “begar” (a form of forced labour without payment), and other similar forms of forced labour; any contravention of this provision is an offence punishable in accordance with law. 2. This right is available against both the State and private individuals, and is enforced through legislation such as the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956 and the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976. 3. In People’s Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982), the Supreme Court held that “forced labour” under Article 23 includes not only physical/legal compulsion but also labour rendered for remuneration less than the minimum wage, since a person forced by economic necessity to work for less than minimum wage is effectively subject to a form of “force.” 4. Article 23(2) carves out an exception permitting the State to impose compulsory service for public purposes (e.g., military conscription, social service schemes), provided the State does not discriminate in making such service compulsory on grounds only of religion, race, caste, or class.

Article 24 — Prohibition of Employment of Children in Hazardous Employment: Prohibits the employment of children below the age of 14 years in any factory, mine, or other hazardous employment — reinforced by the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986 (as amended) and linked with Article 21A’s guarantee of free education.

2Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25-28)

Article 25 — Freedom of Conscience and Free Profession, Practice, and Propagation of Religion: - Guarantees to all persons (not just citizens) freedom of conscience and the right to freely profess, practise, and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health, and other Fundamental Rights. - This right does not include a right to convert another person through force, fraud, or allurement (as clarified in Rev. Stainislaus v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1977), which upheld state anti-conversion laws penalizing forcible/fraudulent conversion). - The Explanation to Article 25 clarifies that the wearing/carrying of kirpans is deemed to be included in the profession of the Sikh religion, and that reference to “Hindus” includes persons professing the Sikh, Jain, or Buddhist religion for the purposes of this Article (relevant to laws on social welfare/reform affecting Hindu-law-governed communities). - The State retains the power to regulate/restrict any economic, financial, political, or other secular activity associated with religious practice, and to provide for social welfare/reform (e.g., opening Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes/sections of Hindus, addressing untouchability-related exclusions).

Article 26 — Freedom to Manage Religious Affairs: Subject to public order, morality, and health, every religious denomination (or any section thereof) has the right to (a) establish and maintain institutions for religious/charitable purposes, (b) manage its own affairs in matters of religion, (c) own and acquire movable/immovable property, and (d) administer such property in accordance with law.

Article 27 — Freedom from Payment of Taxes for Promotion of a Religion: No person can be compelled to pay any tax the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated for the promotion/maintenance of any particular religion/religious denomination (this does not prohibit a general “fee” for services rendered, as distinct from a “tax”).

Article 28 — Freedom from Religious Instruction in Certain Educational Institutions: No religious instruction shall be provided in any educational institution wholly maintained out of State funds; this restriction does not apply to institutions administered by the State but established under an endowment/trust requiring religious instruction, nor does it prevent students in State-recognized (but not State-funded/administered) institutions from attending religious instruction with their (or their guardian’s) consent.

3Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29-30)

Article 29 — Protection of Interests of Minorities: 1. Article 29(1): Any section of citizens residing in India having a distinct language, script, or culture of its own has the right to conserve the same. 2. Article 29(2): No citizen shall be denied admission into any State-maintained or State-aided educational institution on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language, or any of them.

Article 30 — Right of Minorities to Establish and Administer Educational Institutions: 1. Article 30(1): All minorities, whether based on religion or language, have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. 2. Article 30(2): The State shall not, in granting aid to educational institutions, discriminate against any institution on the ground that it is under the management of a minority. 3. This right has been the subject of extensive litigation regarding the extent of state regulation permissible over minority institutions (e.g., regarding admission procedures, fee structures, and staff recruitment) without infringing the core of the “right to administer” — courts have generally held that reasonable regulations in the interest of academic standards and administrative efficiency are permissible, but regulation cannot be so extensive as to destroy the minority character or autonomy of the institution.

4Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32) and Writs

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights conferred by Part III, and empowers the Supreme Court to issue directions, orders, or writs (including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and certiorari) for the enforcement of these rights.

Significance: Dr. B.R. Ambedkar famously described Article 32 as “the heart and soul of the Constitution”, since without an effective remedy to enforce them, Fundamental Rights would be reduced to mere paper declarations. Notably, the right guaranteed by Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right, meaning Parliament cannot ordinarily take away the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction to entertain such petitions (except as expressly provided in the Constitution, such as during specific emergency conditions under Article 359).

The Five Writs: 1. Habeas Corpus (“to have the body”): Directs that a person who has been detained be produced before the court, to examine the legality of the detention; if the detention is found unlawful, the court orders the person’s release. 2. Mandamus (“we command”): A command issued to a public authority to perform a public/statutory duty which it has failed or refused to perform. 3. Prohibition: Issued by a higher court to a lower court/tribunal to prevent it from exceeding its jurisdiction or acting contrary to the rules of natural justice, in a matter still pending before it. 4. Certiorari (“to be certified/informed”): Issued to quash an order already passed by a lower court/tribunal/authority which acted without or in excess of jurisdiction, or in violation of natural justice, or contains an error apparent on the face of the record. 5. Quo Warranto (“by what authority”): Issued to question the legal authority/right of a person to hold a public office, and to oust them from that office if they lack the requisite legal qualification/authority.

Note: While Article 32 confines this remedy to the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights only, Article 226 confers a parallel (and even wider) power on the High Courts, which can issue similar writs not only for enforcement of Fundamental Rights but also “for any other purpose” (i.e., enforcement of ordinary legal rights).

5Saving of Certain Laws (Articles 31A, 31B, 31C)

These provisions were introduced (primarily through the 1st, 4th, and 25th Amendments) to protect certain categories of legislation — particularly agrarian and socio-economic reform measures — from being struck down as violative of Fundamental Rights (especially Articles 14, 19, and the former Article 31 on the right to property):

  1. Article 31A: Saves five categories of laws from challenge on the ground of inconsistency with Articles 14 and 19 — most importantly, laws providing for the acquisition of estates (agrarian reform legislation, zamindari abolition, etc.), and laws relating to the management of properties temporarily taken over in the public interest, amalgamation of corporations, and extinguishment/modification of rights of directors/shareholders of corporations, and rights arising from mining leases.
  2. Article 31B: Provides that acts and regulations specified in the Ninth Schedule to the Constitution shall not be deemed void on the ground that they are inconsistent with, or take away/abridge, any Fundamental Right, and this immunity applies notwithstanding any judgment/decree/order of any court to the contrary. However, in I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), the Supreme Court held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 (the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment) are not automatically immune from judicial review, and remain open to challenge if they violate the basic structure of the Constitution or the Fundamental Rights forming part of that basic structure.
  3. Article 31C: Provides that no law giving effect to the Directive Principles specified in Article 39(b) and (c) (relating to equitable distribution of material resources and prevention of concentration of wealth) shall be deemed void merely because it is inconsistent with, or takes away/abridges, Articles 14 or 19; it also originally sought to bar judicial review of a legislative declaration that a law was for giving effect to such policy, but this latter part (ousting judicial review of the declaration itself) was struck down in Kesavananda Bharati as violative of the basic structure. A subsequent attempt to extend Article 31C’s protection to laws giving effect to any of the Directive Principles (not just 39(b)/(c)) was struck down in Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) as destroying the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles, itself a part of the basic structure.
6Effect of Emergency on Fundamental Rights (Articles 358 and 359)
  1. Article 358: Provides that when a proclamation of National Emergency (under Article 352) is made on the ground of war or external aggression, the six freedoms guaranteed under Article 19 are automatically suspended for the duration of the emergency (the 44th Amendment restricted this automatic suspension to emergencies declared on the ground of war/external aggression, excluding “armed rebellion,” and further clarified that laws made during the emergency which are inconsistent with Article 19 cease to have effect as soon as the emergency ends, to the extent of the inconsistency, though this does not affect anything done/omitted during the period the law was in force).
  2. Article 359: Empowers the President, during any proclamation of emergency (whether declared on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion), to issue an order suspending the right to move any court for the enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights (other than Articles 20 and 21, which the 44th Amendment expressly excluded from ever being suspended, in response to the widely criticized suspension of even Articles 20/21 during the 1975-77 Emergency, as controversially upheld in ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976), a decision now considered bad law and effectively overruled by the subsequent constitutional amendment and later judicial pronouncements including K.S. Puttaswamy).
  3. Key Distinction: Article 358 causes an automatic, blanket suspension of Article 19 rights themselves (only during war/external aggression emergencies); Article 359, by contrast, does not suspend the Fundamental Rights themselves but only suspends the right to enforce specified rights through courts, for the duration specified in the Presidential order, and requires a specific Presidential order naming the rights so affected.

Unit-V: Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties

1. Directive Principles of State Policy — Meaning, Significance and Nature 2. Classification of Directive Principles 3. Application and Judicial Interpretation of Directive Principles 4. Relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles 5. Fundamental Duties (Article 51A) — Significance 6. Enforceability and Judicial Interpretation of Fundamental Duties
1Directive Principles of State Policy — Meaning, Significance and Nature

Meaning: The Directive Principles of State Policy, contained in Part IV (Articles 36-51) of the Constitution, are a set of directions/instructions to the State (both Union and State governments) to guide the framing of laws and policies aimed at establishing social and economic democracy, complementing the political democracy secured through Fundamental Rights.

Nature — Non-Justiciability: Article 37 expressly declares that the Directive Principles “shall not be enforceable by any court,” but nevertheless states that they are “fundamental in the governance of the country” and that it shall be the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws. Thus, while a citizen cannot approach a court to directly enforce a Directive Principle (unlike a Fundamental Right), they represent binding directions of a moral and political character that a State is expected to progressively implement.

Significance: The Directive Principles were inspired substantially by the Irish Constitution (which itself borrowed the concept from Spain) and reflect the framers’ vision of a welfare state, aiming to secure social and economic justice, going beyond the purely negative, individual-liberty-oriented character of many Fundamental Rights (particularly as originally conceived) to affirmatively direct the State towards reducing inequality and improving the material and social conditions of the people.

2Classification of Directive Principles

Though not explicitly classified in the constitutional text, commentators generally group the Directive Principles into three broad categories based on their underlying philosophy:

  1. Socialistic Principles: Directing the State towards securing social and economic justice and reducing inequality — e.g., Article 38 (State to secure a social order for promotion of welfare of the people), Article 39 (certain principles of policy including equal pay for equal work, adequate means of livelihood, distribution of material resources to sub-serve the common good, and prevention of concentration of wealth), Article 39A (equal justice and free legal aid), Article 41 (right to work, education, and public assistance in certain cases), Article 42 (just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief), and Article 43 (living wage for workers).
  2. Gandhian Principles: Reflecting Gandhian ideology and the vision of a decentralized, self-reliant village-based economy — e.g., Article 40 (organization of village panchayats), Article 46 (promotion of educational and economic interests of Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and other weaker sections), Article 47 (duty of the State to raise the level of nutrition, standard of living, and public health, and to prohibit consumption of intoxicating drinks/drugs injurious to health), and Article 48 (organization of agriculture and animal husbandry on modern, scientific lines, including preservation and improvement of breeds and prohibition of slaughter of cows, calves, and other milch/draught cattle).
  3. Liberal-Intellectual Principles: Reflecting broadly liberal, internationalist, and constitutionalist ideals — e.g., Article 44 (uniform civil code for citizens throughout India), Article 45 (early childhood care and education for children below the age of 6, as amended by the 86th Amendment), Article 48A (protection and improvement of environment and safeguarding of forests and wildlife, added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976), Article 49 (protection of monuments and places/objects of national importance), Article 50 (separation of judiciary from the executive in public services of the State), and Article 51 (promotion of international peace and security, and encouragement of settlement of international disputes by arbitration).
3Application and Judicial Interpretation of Directive Principles

Judicial Approach: Although courts cannot directly enforce Directive Principles, they have played a significant interpretive role by using the Directive Principles to inform the meaning and scope of Fundamental Rights and other legal provisions:

  1. Courts have used Directive Principles as an aid to construing ambiguous statutory or constitutional provisions, preferring an interpretation that advances the Directive Principles over one that defeats them.
  2. In several landmark cases, the Supreme Court has read specific Directive Principles into the ambit of Article 21 (right to life), effectively giving them enforceable content — e.g., the right to a clean environment (linked to Article 48A), the right to education (which led ultimately to Article 21A being added as a Fundamental Right, reflecting the earlier Directive Principle in Article 45), and the right to free legal aid (linked to Article 39A) as recognized in Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) and M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra (1978).
  3. Legislation implementing specific Directive Principles (e.g., land reform laws implementing Article 39(b)-(c), the Right to Education Act implementing Article 21A/45, labour welfare laws implementing Articles 42-43) demonstrates the practical, progressive translation of these non-justiciable principles into concrete, judicially enforceable statutory rights over time.
4Relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

The relationship between Part III (Fundamental Rights) and Part IV (Directive Principles) has evolved considerably through constitutional history:

  1. Early Conflict: In State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951), the Supreme Court held that in case of any conflict between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles, Fundamental Rights would prevail, since Directive Principles were expressly made non-justiciable and subordinate to Fundamental Rights under the original constitutional scheme (Article 37 stating that Directive Principles shall not be enforceable by any court). This decision led directly to the 1st Constitutional Amendment (1951), which added Article 15(4) (enabling reservations) to accommodate the Directive Principle in Article 46.
  2. Attempts to Give Primacy to Directive Principles: Later amendments (particularly Article 31C, discussed above) attempted to give overriding effect to laws implementing certain Directive Principles over Articles 14 and 19.
  3. Harmonious Construction — the Modern Position: In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) and subsequently in Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), the Supreme Court settled the relationship by holding that Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are not mutually exclusive but are complementary to one another, together constituting the core of the Constitution’s commitment to social revolution — neither part has absolute primacy over the other, and Parliament’s power to amend the Constitution to implement Directive Principles cannot be exercised so as to damage or destroy the “basic structure,” including the harmony/balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles itself. This “harmonious construction” approach is now the settled constitutional position.
5Fundamental Duties (Article 51A) — Significance

Background: Fundamental Duties were not part of the original Constitution of 1950; they were added by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976, based on the recommendations of the Swaran Singh Committee, drawing partial inspiration from the Constitution of the erstwhile USSR. A tenth duty (relating to the duty of parents/guardians to provide educational opportunities to their children between the ages of 6-14) was later added by the 86th Amendment, 2002, alongside the introduction of Article 21A.

The Ten (now Eleven) Duties [Article 51A] include, among others: to abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag, and the National Anthem; to cherish and follow the noble ideals of the freedom struggle; to uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity, and integrity of India; to defend the country and render national service when called upon; to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all the people of India, transcending religious, linguistic, and regional or sectional diversities, and to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women; to value and preserve the rich heritage of the composite culture; to protect and improve the natural environment and have compassion for living creatures; to develop scientific temper, humanism, and the spirit of inquiry and reform; to safeguard public property and abjure violence; to strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity; and (added in 2002) the duty of a parent/guardian to provide educational opportunities to their child/ward between 6-14 years of age.

Significance: The Fundamental Duties serve to remind citizens that along with the enjoyment of Fundamental Rights, they also owe certain basic obligations to the nation and to fellow citizens, reflecting the idea that rights and duties are correlative, and reinforcing constitutional morality and civic responsibility as a two-way relationship between the citizen and the State.

6Enforceability and Judicial Interpretation of Fundamental Duties

Non-Justiciability: Like the Directive Principles, the Fundamental Duties under Article 51A are not directly enforceable by courts — there is no constitutional provision for legal sanction to compel citizens to comply with them, and no court can strike down a law or issue a mandamus solely on the ground that a person or the State has failed to perform a Fundamental Duty.

Indirect Judicial Use: Nevertheless, courts have used Fundamental Duties as an interpretative aid and as a factor to be considered while adjudging the reasonableness of restrictions on Fundamental Rights or while examining the validity of legislation, in a manner similar to their use of the Directive Principles: 1. In cases concerning environmental protection, courts have relied on the duty under Article 51A(g) (to protect and improve the natural environment) alongside Article 48A (the corresponding Directive Principle) to support judicial intervention in environmental matters (e.g., in the various M.C. Mehta environmental litigation cases). 2. Courts have referred to Article 51A(e) (renouncing practices derogatory to the dignity of women) while adjudicating on issues of gender justice and women’s rights. 3. Parliament has, in some instances, enacted legislation to give concrete shape to certain duties — for example, laws relating to protection of national symbols (flag, anthem) draw upon the duty in Article 51A(a).

Overall Assessment: While Fundamental Duties remain non-justiciable in the strict legal sense, they carry significant moral and constitutional value, and Indian courts have progressively used them (much like Directive Principles) as an aid in constitutional interpretation, thereby giving them an indirect, persuasive form of legal relevance despite their non-enforceable character.
