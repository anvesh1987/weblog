Unit-I: Sources and Schools of Hindu Law; Joint Family, Coparcenary and Karta

1. Meaning, Nature and Application of Hindu Law 2. Sources of Hindu Law 3. Schools of Hindu Law 4. Joint Hindu Family 5. Coparcenary and Coparcenary Property 6. Karta - Position, Powers and Functions 7. Doctrine of Pious Obligation 8. Partition 9. Debts and Alienation of Joint Family Property
1Meaning, Nature and Application of Hindu Law

Hindu Law is one of the oldest personal law systems in the world, applicable to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs domiciled in India (and, for certain purposes, outside India), as defined under Section 2 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 and the other Hindu Code Acts of 1956. It also applies to any person who is not a Muslim, Christian, Parsi or Jew, unless it is proved that such person would not have been governed by Hindu Law had the Acts not been passed.

Historically regarded as a branch of Dharma (righteous duty) rather than positive law in the Austinian sense, Hindu Law has been progressively codified since 1955-56 through the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, and the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 (together often called the 'Hindu Code'). Where the codified law is silent, the old textual and customary law continues to apply as a residuary source.

2Sources of Hindu Law

Sources are conventionally classified as Ancient and Modern sources.

  • Shruti - literally 'that which is heard'; the four Vedas (Rig, Yajur, Sama, Atharva) containing religious and moral precepts; regarded as the primary and most authoritative source, though of limited direct legal content.
  • Smriti - 'that which is remembered'; texts composed by sages recollecting the Vedas, including the Dharmasutras and Dharmashastras (e.g., Manusmriti, Yajnavalkya Smriti, Narada Smriti). These contain the bulk of substantive legal rules on family, property and succession.
  • Commentaries and Digests (Nibandhas) - written between the 7th and 12th centuries CE to explain and reconcile conflicting Smritis; it is these commentaries, rather than the Smritis themselves, that gave rise to the different schools of Hindu Law (e.g., Mitakshara by Vijnaneshwara, Dayabhaga by Jimutavahana).
  • Custom - a rule which, by long and continuous usage, has obtained the force of law in a particular family, caste, community or locality; to be legally binding a custom must be ancient, certain, reasonable, continuous and not opposed to public policy or expressly abolished by statute. Customs are of four kinds: local, family, class/caste, and guild (trade) customs.
  • Modern sources - Justice, Equity and Good Conscience (applied where no specific rule exists); Legislation (the codifying Acts of 1955-56 and subsequent amendments); and Judicial Precedent (decisions of the Supreme Court, binding under Article 141 of the Constitution, and of the High Courts).
3Schools of Hindu Law

The divergent interpretations placed on the Smritis by different commentators gave rise to two principal schools: the Mitakshara school and the Dayabhaga school. Broadly, the Mitakshara school prevails throughout India except in Bengal and Assam, where the Dayabhaga school historically prevailed.

  • Mitakshara School - based on Vijnaneshwara's commentary on the Yajnavalkya Smriti; recognises the concept of a son's right by birth in ancestral/coparcenary property; has four sub-schools with local variations - Benares, Mithila, Maharashtra (Bombay), and Dravida (Madras) schools.
  • Dayabhaga School - based on Jimutavahana's treatise; does not recognise a right by birth; a son acquires an interest in the family property only on the death of the father, who has absolute power of disposal over the property during his lifetime.
  • Key differences: under Mitakshara, coparcenary interest arises by birth and property devolves by survivorship among coparceners; under Dayabhaga, no interest arises by birth, the father is the absolute owner during his life, and on his death the property devolves by inheritance (not survivorship) even among sons, giving each son an independently alienable and inheritable share.
  • Following the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005, which made daughters coparceners by birth (see Unit V), and other statutory reforms, the practical significance of the Mitakshara/Dayabhaga distinction has considerably narrowed, though it remains conceptually important.
4Joint Hindu Family

A Joint Hindu Family is the normal condition of Hindu society and consists of all persons lineally descended from a common ancestor, including their wives and unmarried daughters. It is larger than a coparcenary and may include several generations. Membership in a joint family does not depend on possession of property - a family can be joint in food, worship and estate even without any property, though most litigation concerns joint family property.

A Hindu joint family is presumed to continue joint unless the contrary (i.e., partition) is proved; the burden of proving separation lies on the party who asserts it.

5Coparcenary and Coparcenary Property

A coparcenary is a narrower body within the joint family, consisting of persons who acquire an interest in the joint family property by birth and who have a right to demand partition. Traditionally, only male lineal descendants up to four degrees from a common male ancestor (father, son, grandson, great-grandson) were coparceners; this position has been altered by the 2005 Amendment giving daughters coparcenary status by birth.

  • Coparcenary property (ancestral property) includes: (i) property inherited by a Hindu from his father, father's father, or father's father's father; (ii) property acquired with the aid of joint family funds; and (iii) property thrown into the common stock by a coparcener.
  • Separate/self-acquired property is property acquired by a coparcener through his own efforts, without detriment to joint family property (e.g., by personal skill, gift, or government grant), and remains his exclusive property unless voluntarily blended with joint family property.
  • Incidents of coparcenary property: community of interest and unity of possession among coparceners; devolution by survivorship (subject to the 2005 Amendment which substituted testamentary/intestate succession for a deceased coparcener's interest); and the right of every coparcener to demand partition.
6Karta - Position, Powers and Functions

The Karta is the senior-most male (or, after the 2005 Amendment and judicial recognition, senior-most female coparcener) member of the joint family who manages the family's affairs and property. The Karta's position is sui generis - not a partner, agent or trustee in the strict legal sense, but a manager with unique fiduciary responsibilities.

  • Powers of management: the Karta has wide powers over the management of joint family property and business, and is not accountable for mere errors of judgment made in good faith.
  • Power of alienation: as a general rule, the Karta cannot alienate joint family property except for (i) legal necessity, (ii) benefit of the estate, or (iii) discharge of indispensable religious/pious duties; an alienation without these justifications is voidable at the instance of the other coparceners.
  • Power to contract debts: the Karta may contract debts for family purposes, and such debts bind the shares of all coparceners, subject to the doctrine of pious obligation.
  • Power to represent the family in litigation and to enter into compromises and family settlements for the family's benefit.
  • Duties: to maintain accounts (though not bound to save or invest), to maintain all members of the family, to marry off daughters, and to act in the interests of the whole family rather than for personal gain.
7Doctrine of Pious Obligation

Under classical Hindu Law, a son is under a moral and legal obligation to pay off his father's debts, provided the debts are not avyavaharik (immoral, illegal, or incurred for illegitimate purposes, such as debts for drinking, gambling or promoting adultery). This is known as the doctrine of pious obligation, and it renders the son's (and, in unbroken succession, grandson's and great-grandson's) interest in the coparcenary property liable for the father's debts, even though the son did not personally contract them.

The doctrine is founded on the religious belief that a son who does not discharge his father's debts causes the father spiritual detriment; it has been substantially curtailed by Section 6(4) of the Hindu Succession Act (as inserted by the 2005 Amendment), which abolishes the son's pious obligation to pay a father's debts contracted after the commencement of the amendment, though it preserves the enforceability of debts contracted before that date.

8Partition

Partition is the crystallisation of the joint status into separate shares; it converts joint tenancy (coparcenary ownership) into ownership in severalty. It has two aspects: (i) severance of status (a mere declaration of intention to separate, which is complete the moment the intention is unequivocally expressed and communicated, even before actual division of properties by metes and bounds), and (ii) actual division of property by metes and bounds.

  • Modes of partition: by agreement among coparceners; by unilateral declaration/notice by a coparcener demanding his share; by arbitration; by suit for partition; and, in some cases, by conduct evidencing an intention to separate.
  • Persons entitled to demand partition: every adult coparcener; a minor coparcener through his guardian, if partition is in his interest; and, after the 2005 Amendment, a daughter as a coparcener in her own right.
  • Effect of partition: each coparcener's undivided share becomes an absolute, separate and heritable property; the joint family, as regards the separated members, comes to an end, though the remaining members may continue joint inter se.
  • Reunion: Hindu Law recognises the possibility of reunion after partition, but only among specified persons (father, son, brothers who were parties to the original partition) and only where there is a clear intention to reunite in estate and interest, not merely to live together again.
9Debts and Alienation of Joint Family Property

Because the Karta's power to alienate joint family property is limited, the law developed the twin concepts of legal necessity and benefit of the estate to test the validity of alienations.

  • Legal necessity - includes payment of government revenue, maintenance of family members, marriage expenses of coparceners' children (particularly daughters), funeral and religious ceremonies, defence in criminal or civil litigation concerning the family, and payment of antecedent debts.
  • Benefit of the estate - broadly interpreted by courts to include any transaction that is of positive advantage to the family, not confined to purely defensive or protective acts, but excluding speculative or reckless transactions.
  • Antecedent debt - a debt which is prior in time and dissociated from the alienation in question; an alienation to discharge a genuinely antecedent debt (not incurred for an immoral purpose) can bind the shares of the sons under the doctrine of pious obligation, even without independent legal necessity.
  • An alienation made without legal necessity, benefit of estate, or for discharge of antecedent debt is not void, but voidable at the option of the non-consenting coparceners, who may challenge it in a suit for partition or for a declaration that it does not bind their shares.

Unit-II: Hindu Marriage

1. Meaning, Nature and Importance of Marriage under Hindu Law 2. Conditions for a Valid Hindu Marriage (Section 5, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955) 3. Ceremonies of Marriage and Registration 4. Monogamy and Polygamy 5. Recent Trends in the Institution of Marriage
1Meaning, Nature and Importance of Marriage under Hindu Law

Under classical Hindu Law, marriage (Vivaha) was regarded as a sacrament (samskara) - a sacred, indissoluble union essential for the performance of religious duties and the discharge of three debts (rin) owed to the gods, sages and ancestors - rather than a contract. The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 has, however, introduced strong contractual elements (conditions for validity, void and voidable marriages, divorce) while retaining ceremonial and sacramental features, so that modern Hindu marriage is often described as sacramental in origin but contractual in incidents.

The institution of marriage remains central to Hindu family law as the foundation of the joint family, legitimate succession, and the mutual rights and obligations (maintenance, cohabitation, fidelity) between spouses.

2Conditions for a Valid Hindu Marriage (Section 5, Hindu Marriage Act, 1955)

Section 5 lays down cumulative conditions, the breach of some of which renders the marriage void, and of others voidable or merely punishable.

  • Monogamy - neither party should have a spouse living at the time of marriage (Section 5(i)); violation renders the marriage void under Section 11, and is also punishable as bigamy.
  • Soundness of mind - neither party should be incapable of giving valid consent due to unsoundness of mind, or though capable of giving consent, suffering from a mental disorder unfit for marriage and procreation of children, or subject to recurrent attacks of insanity (Section 5(ii)).
  • Age of marriage - the bridegroom must have completed 21 years and the bride 18 years at the time of marriage (Section 5(iii)); a marriage in contravention of this condition is neither void nor voidable but is a punishable offence, and is also addressed by the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006.
  • Prohibited degrees of relationship - the parties should not be within the degrees of prohibited relationship (as defined in Section 3) unless a custom or usage governing each of them permits such a marriage (Section 5(iv)).
  • Sapinda relationship - the parties should not be sapindas of each other unless permitted by custom or usage (Section 5(v)); sapinda relationship extends up to the third generation in the line of the mother and the fifth in the line of the father, computed through the person concerned.
  • Breach of the prohibited degrees or sapinda conditions renders the marriage void under Section 11, unless saved by custom.
3Ceremonies of Marriage and Registration

Section 7 provides that a Hindu marriage may be solemnised in accordance with the customary rites and ceremonies of either party. Where such rites include the saptapadi (the taking of seven steps by the bridegroom and bride jointly before the sacred fire), the marriage becomes complete and binding when the seventh step is taken.

Section 8 empowers State Governments to make rules for registration of Hindu marriages to facilitate proof of marriage; registration is not a precondition for the validity of a marriage solemnised in accordance with Section 7, but the Supreme Court in Seema v. Ashwani Kumar has directed that registration of marriages of all citizens be made compulsory to serve as prima facie evidence and to protect women's rights.

4Monogamy and Polygamy

Prior to 1955, polygamy (a Hindu man having more than one wife simultaneously) was legally permissible, though socially discouraged in many communities. The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 abolished polygamy by making monogamy a mandatory condition (Section 5(i)) and by declaring a bigamous marriage void (Section 11); it also attracts criminal liability for bigamy.

The transition from a polygamous to a strictly monogamous system is regarded as one of the most significant reforms achieved by codification, aligning Hindu personal law more closely with constitutional guarantees of gender equality, though debates on a Uniform Civil Code and religion-neutral marriage regulation continue.

5Recent Trends in the Institution of Marriage

Contemporary developments relevant to this topic include the increasing judicial and legislative recognition of live-in relationships as a form of relationship 'in the nature of marriage' for limited purposes (e.g., maintenance under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, and Section 125 Cr.P.C./BNSS by extension), growing acceptance of inter-caste and inter-religious marriages (often solemnised or registered under the Special Marriage Act, 1954), rising incidence of separation and divorce by mutual consent, and debates around a Uniform Civil Code. Courts have also increasingly emphasised free consent and the constitutional right to choose one's life partner (Shafin Jahan v. Asokan K.M.; Shakti Vahini v. Union of India, on honour crimes) as facets of personal liberty under Article 21.

Unit-III: Matrimonial Remedies under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955

Divorce by Mutual Consent (Section 13B) 7. Theories of Divorce 8. Maintenance Pendente Lite and Permanent Alimony (Sections 24 and 25) 9. Importance of Conciliation and Role of Family Courts
1Restitution of Conjugal Rights (Section 9)

Where either spouse has, without reasonable excuse, withdrawn from the society of the other, the aggrieved party may apply to the District Court for restitution of conjugal rights, and the court, if satisfied of the truth of the statements and that there is no legal ground why the relief should not be granted, may decree restitution. This remedy assumes that marriage imposes a mutual obligation of cohabitation.

The constitutional validity of Section 9 was challenged as violative of the right to privacy and bodily autonomy in T. Sareetha v. Venkata Subbaiah (Andhra Pradesh High Court, holding it unconstitutional) but was upheld by the Delhi High Court in Harvinder Kaur v. Harmander Singh, and subsequently by the Supreme Court in Saroj Rani v. Sudarshan Kumar Chadha, which held the provision constitutional, though the debate about its compatibility with personal liberty continues academically. Non-compliance with a decree for restitution for one year or more is itself a ground for divorce under Section 13(1A)(ii).

2Void Marriages (Section 11)

A marriage is void ab initio (i.e., no marriage in the eyes of law at all) if it contravenes any of the conditions specified in clauses (i), (iv) or (v) of Section 5 - namely bigamy, prohibited degrees of relationship, or sapinda relationship (without a saving custom). Either party may obtain a decree of nullity, though such a decree is only declaratory, since a void marriage confers no marital status from the outset. Children of a void marriage are, however, legitimate for purposes of inheriting from their parents by virtue of Section 16 of the Act.

3Voidable Marriages (Section 12)

A voidable marriage remains valid and binding until it is annulled by a competent court on the petition of one of the parties; it cannot be treated as void by the parties themselves.

  • Grounds: (a) inability of the respondent to consummate the marriage due to impotence; (b) contravention of the condition regarding unsoundness of mind or mental disorder in Section 5(ii); (c) consent of the petitioner (or of the guardian, where consent of a guardian was required) obtained by force or fraud as to the nature of the ceremony or as to any material fact concerning the respondent; (d) the respondent was pregnant by some person other than the petitioner at the time of marriage.
  • Bars to relief - the petition under grounds (c) and (d) must ordinarily be filed within one year of the force/fraud ceasing to operate or of the petitioner's discovery of the pregnancy, and the petitioner must not have voluntarily cohabited with the respondent with full knowledge of the relevant facts.
4Judicial Separation (Section 10)

Either party may present a petition for judicial separation on any of the grounds available for divorce under Section 13(1) (and, for wives, the additional grounds under Section 13(2)). A decree of judicial separation does not dissolve the marriage but relieves the parties of the obligation to cohabit; it operates as a cooling-off remedy, and the parties may resume cohabitation at any time by mutual agreement, rescinding the decree. If cohabitation is not resumed for one year or more after the decree, this itself becomes an independent ground for divorce.

5Divorce - Grounds under Section 13

Section 13(1) sets out grounds available to either spouse, and Section 13(2) sets out additional grounds available exclusively to the wife.

  • Adultery - voluntary sexual intercourse by the respondent with a person other than the spouse after solemnisation of the marriage.
  • Cruelty - conduct (physical or mental) of such a nature that it causes reasonable apprehension in the petitioner's mind that it would be harmful or injurious to live with the respondent; the concept has been given an expansive and evolving interpretation by courts (covering, e.g., persistent taunting, false criminal complaints, denial of matrimonial obligations, and unreasonable demands).
  • Desertion - the intentional permanent abandonment of one spouse by the other without reasonable cause and without consent, for a continuous period of not less than two years immediately preceding the presentation of the petition; requires both the factum of separation and the animus deserendi (intention to desert).
  • Conversion - the respondent has ceased to be a Hindu by conversion to another religion.
  • Unsoundness of mind - the respondent has been incurably of unsound mind, or has been suffering continuously or intermittently from mental disorder of such a kind and to such an extent that the petitioner cannot reasonably be expected to live with the respondent.
  • Virulent and incurable form of leprosy - retained historically as a ground (subject to later amendment removing leprosy in some contexts under general disability law reforms).
  • Venereal disease in a communicable form.
  • Renunciation of the world by entering a religious order.
  • Presumption of death - the respondent has not been heard of as being alive for a period of seven years or more by persons who would naturally have heard of it.
  • Additional grounds available only to the wife (Section 13(2)): pre-Act bigamy of the husband (where he had another wife living at the time of marriage who is still alive); husband guilty of rape, sodomy or bestiality since the solemnisation of the marriage; a decree or order awarding maintenance to the wife under Section 18 of the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 or under Section 125 Cr.P.C./BNSS, followed by non-resumption of cohabitation for a year or more; and repudiation of the marriage (option of puberty) where the marriage was solemnised before the wife attained 15 years of age and she has repudiated it after attaining that age but before 18.
6Divorce by Mutual Consent (Section 13B)

Both parties may jointly present a petition for divorce on the ground that they have been living separately for a period of one year or more, have not been able to live together, and have mutually agreed that the marriage should be dissolved. A mandatory cooling-off period of six to eighteen months between the first and second motions was traditionally required, but the Supreme Court in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur held that this period is directory, not mandatory, and can be waived by courts in appropriate cases (e.g., where reconciliation is clearly impossible and parties have already settled ancillary issues).

7Theories of Divorce

Matrimonial law generally recognises three theories: (i) the fault theory, under which divorce is granted only if one party is guilty of a matrimonial offence (as under most grounds in Section 13(1)); (ii) the consent theory, under which divorce is available where both parties agree (Section 13B); and (iii) the irretrievable breakdown theory, under which a marriage that has broken down beyond any possibility of repair may be dissolved even without proof of fault, on the ground that continuation of the marital tie serves no purpose. India's HMA is primarily fault-based supplemented by mutual consent, but the Supreme Court has, in its inherent jurisdiction under Article 142, dissolved marriages on the ground of irretrievable breakdown in appropriate cases, while consistently recommending that Parliament introduce it as a statutory ground (as recommended by the Law Commission of India in its 71st Report).

8Maintenance Pendente Lite and Permanent Alimony (Sections 24 and 25)

Section 24 empowers the court, in any proceeding under the Act, to order either spouse (who has no independent income sufficient for support and the necessary expenses of the proceeding) to be paid, by the other spouse, the expenses of the proceeding and monthly maintenance during its pendency (maintenance pendente lite), having regard to the petitioner's and respondent's income.

Section 25 empowers the court, at the time of passing any decree or at any time thereafter, to order permanent alimony and maintenance - either a gross sum or periodical payment - to either spouse for life, having regard to the respondent's own income and other property, the applicant's income and property, the conduct of the parties, and other circumstances; such orders may be varied, modified or rescinded on a subsequent change of circumstances.

9Importance of Conciliation and Role of Family Courts

Section 23(2) of the Act casts a duty on the court to make every endeavour, before granting relief, to bring about reconciliation between the parties in any proceeding for restitution of conjugal rights, judicial separation or divorce (except where the ground is one where reconciliation would be inappropriate).

The Family Courts Act, 1984 was enacted to establish Family Courts with the specific object of promoting conciliation and securing speedy settlement of disputes relating to marriage and family affairs. Family Courts follow a simplified procedure, are assisted by counsellors, sit informally, and are enjoined to make efforts for settlement before adjudicating; they have exclusive jurisdiction over matrimonial matters, maintenance, custody of children, and related family disputes in areas where they are established, reflecting a policy shift from adversarial litigation towards conciliatory resolution of family disputes.

Unit-IV: Adoption, Maintenance and Guardianship

1. Concept and Historical Perspective of Adoption 2. Essential Conditions for a Valid Adoption under HAMA, 1956 3. Effects of Adoption 4. Inter-Country Adoption 5. Law of Maintenance under HAMA, 1956 6. Law of Guardianship under the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956
1Concept and Historical Perspective of Adoption

Under classical Hindu Law, adoption (dattaka) served primarily a religious purpose - to secure a son who could perform the father's funeral rites (shraddha) and continue the family line, and only secondarily a secular purpose of providing an heir. Only sons could traditionally be adopted, and elaborate ceremonies (including datta homam, a religious rite) were often required.

The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 (HAMA) reformed and secularised the law: it permits adoption of both sons and daughters, removes the religious ceremony (datta homam) as a mandatory requirement, and lays down clear statutory conditions for a valid adoption, applicable to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs.

2Essential Conditions for a Valid Adoption under HAMA, 1956
  • Capacity of a male Hindu to take in adoption - he must be of sound mind, not a minor, and if he has a living wife, her consent is required (unless she has renounced the world, ceased to be a Hindu, or has been declared unsound of mind by a competent court).
  • Capacity of a female Hindu to take in adoption - after the 2010 amendment, an unmarried woman, a widow, a divorcee, or a married woman whose husband is dead/has renounced the world/ceased to be a Hindu/been declared of unsound mind may adopt in her own right.
  • Capacity to give in adoption - only the father and mother (or, in specified circumstances, the mother or father alone, or a guardian with court permission) can give a child in adoption; consent of both parents is generally required unless one is disqualified.
  • Persons who may be adopted - the child must be a Hindu, not already adopted, not married (unless custom permits), and must be below the age of 15 (unless custom or usage permits adoption of an older child); the adoptive parent must be at least 21 years older than the child of the opposite sex being adopted.
  • Actual giving and taking - the child must be actually transferred from the birth family to the adoptive family, with the intention of transferring the child from one family to another; this is the essential ceremony required by Section 11, and the datta homam ceremony is no longer compulsory.
3Effects of Adoption

Section 12 provides that an adopted child is deemed to be the child of the adoptive parents for all purposes from the date of adoption, and all ties of the child with the birth family are severed and replaced by ties with the adoptive family, subject to certain savings (e.g., the child does not lose the right to marry any person he/she could have married had there been no adoption, and vested property before adoption is not divested).

4Inter-Country Adoption

In-country and inter-country adoptions of Indian children by foreign parents raise concerns of child welfare and trafficking; in Laxmi Kant Pandey v. Union of India, the Supreme Court laid down detailed guidelines and safeguards to be followed in inter-country adoptions, which subsequently informed the establishment of the Central Adoption Resource Authority (CARA) and its regulations, ensuring adoption is conducted through recognised and monitored agencies, with the welfare of the child as the paramount consideration.

5Law of Maintenance under HAMA, 1956

Chapter III of HAMA (Sections 18-22) codifies the obligation to maintain dependants.

  • Maintenance of wife (Section 18) - a Hindu wife is entitled to be maintained by her husband during her lifetime, and may live separately without forfeiting her claim to maintenance on specified grounds, such as desertion, cruelty, the husband suffering from a virulent form of leprosy, the husband having another wife living, the husband keeping a concubine, or the husband ceasing to be a Hindu.
  • Maintenance of children and aged parents (Sections 20-22) - a Hindu is bound to maintain his or her minor children (legitimate or illegitimate) and aged or infirm parents, to the extent that they are unable to maintain themselves out of their own earnings or property; an unmarried daughter's claim extends until she is able to maintain herself.
  • 'Maintenance' is broadly defined under Section 3 to include provision for food, clothing, residence, education, and medical attendance and treatment, and, in case of an unmarried daughter, also her reasonable marriage expenses.
6Law of Guardianship under the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956

The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 (HMGA) supplements (and does not override, except where inconsistent with) the general law of guardianship contained in the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890.

  • Kinds of guardians - natural guardians (recognised by law without any appointment, per Section 6), testamentary guardians (appointed by a Hindu father or mother by will), and guardians appointed or declared by a court under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890.
  • Natural guardians (Section 6) - for a legitimate minor boy or unmarried girl, the father, and after him, the mother; provided that the custody of a child below the age of five shall ordinarily be with the mother; for an illegitimate child, the mother, and after her, the father; for a married minor girl, the husband. The constitutionality and interpretation of the 'father, and after him, the mother' rule has been read down by the Supreme Court in Githa Hariharan v. Reserve Bank of India to mean that the mother can act as natural guardian even during the father's lifetime if he is absent, indifferent, or otherwise unsuited.
  • Welfare of the minor as paramount consideration (Section 13) - notwithstanding anything in the Act, in appointing or declaring a guardian, the welfare of the minor is the paramount consideration, and no person is entitled to guardianship of a minor by virtue of any provision of the Act if the court finds it not for the minor's welfare.
  • Powers of a natural guardian - to do all acts necessary or reasonable and proper for the benefit of the minor or the realisation, protection or benefit of the minor's estate, subject to the restriction that the guardian cannot, without previous permission of the court, mortgage, charge, transfer by sale, gift, exchange or otherwise, or lease for a term exceeding five years (or beyond one year after the minor attains majority) any part of the minor's immovable property; any such disposal without permission is voidable at the instance of the minor.
  • Removal of guardian - a court may remove a guardian, whether natural, testamentary or court-appointed, on grounds such as abuse of trust, failure to perform duties, incapacity, ceasing to be a Hindu, or conviction for an offence implying moral turpitude, always guided by the paramount consideration of the minor's welfare.

1Background and Objects of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956

Before 1956, succession among Hindus was governed by diverse and often uncertain textual rules that differed between the Mitakshara and Dayabhaga schools, and treated male and female heirs very unequally (women typically taking only a limited/life estate). The Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (HSA) sought to codify and reform this law with the object of laying down a uniform and comprehensive system of intestate succession for all Hindus, and to improve, though not yet fully equalise, the position of female heirs; the position was substantially strengthened by the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005.

2Succession to the Property of a Hindu Male Dying Intestate (Sections 8-13)

Section 8 lays down the general order of succession to a Hindu male's separate property (and, after the 2005 Amendment, effectively also his interest in coparcenary property, since Section 6 now provides for devolution by testamentary or intestate succession rather than automatic survivorship where a daughter or other Class I female heir is involved). Heirs are grouped, in order of preference, into: Class I heirs, Class II heirs, Agnates, and Cognates.

  • Class I heirs (Schedule to the Act) - inherit simultaneously and to the exclusion of all other heirs; include son, daughter, widow, mother, son of a pre-deceased son, daughter of a pre-deceased son, son of a pre-deceased daughter, daughter of a pre-deceased daughter, widow of a pre-deceased son, and other specified heirs added by later amendments.
  • Class II heirs (Sections 9-11) - inherit only in the absence of any Class I heir; are further divided into several sub-categories/entries (father; son's daughter's son/daughter, brother, sister; and so on) which take in order, an entry higher in the list excluding a lower entry, and heirs within the same entry taking simultaneously.
  • Agnates (Sections 12-13) - relatives connected wholly through males (by blood or adoption, not necessarily male themselves), who inherit in the absence of Class I and Class II heirs, computed according to the rules of preference laid down in Sections 12-13 (fewer degrees of ascent/descent generally preferred).
  • Cognates - relatives connected but not wholly through males, who inherit last, in the absence of agnates, applying similar rules of computation of degrees.
3Succession to the Property of a Hindu Female Dying Intestate (Sections 15-16)

Section 15 lays down a separate and distinct scheme for a Hindu female's property, broadly preferring her own children and husband first, then her husband's heirs, then her parents, then her father's heirs, and finally her mother's heirs, in that order.

An important exception: property inherited by a Hindu female from her father or mother devolves, in the absence of her own children, upon the heirs of her father (not her husband's heirs); similarly, property inherited from her husband or father-in-law devolves, in the absence of her own children, upon the heirs of the husband. This source-based rule preserves property within the family from which it originally came, in the absence of direct descendants.

4Section 14 - Enlargement of a Hindu Woman's Limited Estate into Absolute Ownership

One of the most transformative provisions of the 1956 Act is Section 14(1), which converts any property possessed by a female Hindu, whether acquired before or after the commencement of the Act, into her absolute property, thereby abolishing the concept of a Hindu woman's limited estate (women's estate) that existed under the old law, where a woman typically held property only for her lifetime with a reversion to the male heirs of the last full owner.

The property must be 'possessed' by the woman (interpreted broadly by courts to include both actual and constructive possession, and even a mere right to maintenance charged on property in some circumstances), and it must have been acquired by her in one of several specified modes (inheritance, partition, gift, or in lieu of maintenance, and so on) - property acquired for the first time by way of gift or will under Section 14(2), with restrictive conditions attached, may however remain a limited estate as per the terms of that gift/will.

5The 2005 Amendment - Daughters as Coparceners

The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 amended Section 6 to provide that, in a joint Hindu family governed by Mitakshara law, the daughter of a coparcener becomes, by birth, a coparcener in her own right in the same manner as a son, with the same rights and liabilities in the coparcenary property, including the right to claim partition and to be allotted a share on partition, and is subject to the same liabilities (including the modified pious obligation).

  • Retrospective effect and applicability - the Supreme Court, in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020), settled earlier conflicting decisions (Prakash v. Phulavati and Danamma @ Suman Surpur v. Amar) by holding that the 2005 Amendment confers coparcenary status on a daughter by birth, irrespective of whether her father was alive on the date of the amendment (i.e., the daughter's right is by birth and does not depend on the father being a living coparcener as of 9 September 2005), overruling the requirement in Prakash v. Phulavati that the father must be alive on that date.
  • The Amendment does not, however, reopen partitions that had already taken place before 20 December 2004 by a registered deed or a decree of a court.
  • Notional partition (deemed partition) - under the pre-2005 scheme of Section 6, where a male Hindu died leaving surviving him a female relative specified in Class I or a male relative claiming through such a female, his interest in the coparcenary property was deemed, immediately before his death, to have been divided as if a partition had taken place (a legal fiction called notional partition) solely for the purpose of ascertaining his share, which then devolved by succession under Sections 8-13; the shares of the other coparceners in the notional partition were not actually altered.
6Dwelling House (Section 23 - as it stood prior to its omission in 2005)

Prior to its omission by the 2005 Amendment, Section 23 restricted a female heir's right to claim partition of a dwelling house wholly occupied by members of the joint family until the male heirs chose to divide their respective shares; she was, however, entitled to a right of residence in the house. The 2005 Amendment removed this restriction entirely, placing female heirs on an equal footing with male heirs even in respect of the family dwelling house.

7Classes of Heirs and General Rules of Succession - Summary

The overall scheme, therefore, moves through successive classes: for a Hindu male, Class I heirs first, then Class II heirs, then agnates, then cognates (Sections 8-13); for a Hindu female, her own children and husband, then husband's heirs, then parents, then father's heirs, then mother's heirs, subject to the source-based exception in Section 15(2) (Sections 15-16). General rules under Sections 18-30 govern matters such as full-blood being preferred to half-blood in some cases, a person's disqualification for murder or conversion (Sections 25-26), and the devolution of an interest of a person who is not entitled to succeed.

8Daughter's Right to Inherit Ancestral Property and Recent Developments

The cumulative effect of the 2005 Amendment and its interpretation in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma is that a daughter today has an equal and unqualified right, by birth, to ancestral (coparcenary) property, on par with a son - she can demand partition, is entitled to an equal share, and continues to be a coparcener even after marriage. Courts have also had to work out the practical mechanics of implementing these rights in ongoing or previously decided partition suits, and to address related issues such as the impact of the Amendment on daughters who died before 2005 (whose legal representatives may claim a share, as clarified in subsequent case law), demonstrating the continuing evolution of this branch of the law towards full gender equality in succession.
