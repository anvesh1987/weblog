1Meaning and Definition of Environment

The word ‘environment’ derives from the French word environner, meaning ‘to encircle’ or ‘to surround’. In its general sense, environment denotes the sum total of all surroundings – physical, biological, social and cultural – that affect the life and development of an organism.

Section 2(a) of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 defines ‘environment’ to include water, air and land, and the inter-relationship which exists among and between water, air and land, and human beings, other living creatures, plants, micro-organisms and property. This is a wide, inclusive definition covering both abiotic components (air, water, soil, minerals) and biotic components (plants, animals, micro-organisms, humans), and, importantly, the inter-relationships among them. Environment operates at multiple scales – the micro-environment (immediate surroundings) and the macro-environment (global systems).

2Ecology

Ecology is the scientific study of the relationships between living organisms and their environment, a term coined by the German biologist Ernst Haeckel in 1866 from the Greek oikos (house) and logos (study). Ecological organisation proceeds through levels – individual, population, community, ecosystem, biome and biosphere.

  • Habitat – the natural home or place of an organism.
  • Niche – the functional role of an organism within its habitat.
  • Food chain and food web – the transfer of energy from producers to consumers.
  • Trophic levels – producers, primary/secondary/tertiary consumers, and decomposers.
  • Ecological balance – the state of dynamic equilibrium within a community where species composition remains relatively stable.
3Ecosystem

An ecosystem is a structural and functional unit of nature comprising living organisms (the biotic community) and their non-living physical environment (abiotic factors), interacting together as a system – a term coined by A.G. Tansley in 1935. Its abiotic components include sunlight, temperature, water, soil and minerals, while its biotic components include producers (autotrophs), consumers (heterotrophs) and decomposers (saprotrophs).

Ecosystems may be natural (forest, grassland, desert, freshwater or marine) or artificial/man-made (agricultural land, aquarium, urban ecosystem). Their key functions are energy flow, nutrient/biogeochemical cycling (carbon, nitrogen, water cycles) and the maintenance of ecological balance. Ecosystem degradation – deforestation, wetland destruction – is now approached in law through the ‘ecosystem approach’, notably under the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 and wetland/coastal regulations.

4Biosphere

The biosphere is the global sum of all ecosystems – the life-supporting zone of the earth where the lithosphere (land), hydrosphere (water) and atmosphere (air) meet and interact to support life. It is a narrow zone, extending only a few kilometres above and below sea level, within which life exists.

The biosphere is a closed system for matter, since matter is continuously recycled, but an open system for energy, since solar energy enters continuously and heat is ultimately lost. It forms the conceptual basis for the ‘common heritage of mankind’ and ‘common concern of humankind’ principles in international environmental law.

5Biomes

Biomes are large-scale ecological communities of flora and fauna occupying major habitats, characterised by climate, vegetation type and geography. The major biomes are the tropical rainforest, temperate forest, grassland/savannah, desert, taiga (coniferous forest), tundra, and aquatic biomes (freshwater and marine).

India spans several biomes and biogeographic zones – Himalayan, Gangetic plain, Deccan plateau, Western Ghats, coastal, desert (Thar), and island ecosystems – which is why India is recognised as one of the world’s mega-biodiverse countries.

6Ozone Depletion

The ozone layer is a layer of ozone (O₃) in the stratosphere, roughly 15–35 km above the earth, that absorbs most of the sun’s harmful ultraviolet (UV-B) radiation. Its depletion is caused mainly by chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), halons, carbon tetrachloride, methyl bromide and other ozone-depleting substances (ODS) used in refrigerants, aerosols, fire extinguishers and industrial solvents, which release chlorine and bromine atoms that catalytically destroy ozone molecules.

The severe seasonal thinning of ozone observed over Antarctica since the mid-1980s is known as the ‘ozone hole’. Increased UV-B radiation reaching the earth causes skin cancer, cataracts and immune suppression in humans, and damages phytoplankton, crops and marine ecosystems. The international response has been the Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer, 1985 and the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer, 1987 – a landmark and largely successful treaty phasing out ODS.

7Global Warming

Global warming is the long-term rise in the average temperature of the earth’s atmosphere and oceans caused by an increased concentration of greenhouse gases (GHGs). The greenhouse effect is itself a natural process by which GHGs – CO₂, methane, nitrous oxide, water vapour, CFCs – trap heat in the atmosphere; global warming refers to the enhancement of this effect due to anthropogenic emissions.

Its principal causes are the burning of fossil fuels, deforestation, industrialisation, vehicular emissions, agriculture (methane from livestock and paddy fields) and land-use change, while its effects include rising sea levels, melting glaciers and polar ice, extreme weather events, loss of biodiversity, threats to food and water security, and displacement of populations as ‘climate refugees’. The legal and policy response – the UNFCCC (1992), the Kyoto Protocol (1997) and the Paris Agreement (2015) – is discussed in Unit V.

8Climatic Changes

Climate change refers to long-term shifts in temperature, precipitation, wind patterns and other elements of climate, whether due to natural variability or human activity. ‘Weather’ is short-term and local, ‘climate’ is the long-term pattern, and ‘climate change’ denotes statistically significant variation in climate persisting over an extended period.

Its consequences for India include altered monsoon patterns (critical to agriculture), desertification, and increased frequency of cyclones, floods and droughts, along with a broader threat to coastal and island nations worldwide.

9Need for the Preservation, Conservation and Protection of the Environment

Preservation denotes keeping natural resources in their pristine, untouched state; conservation denotes the planned and sustainable management or use of natural resources so as to prevent their exploitation, waste and destruction; and protection denotes active legal and administrative measures to prevent and remedy environmental harm.

  • Survival and health of present and future generations (intergenerational equity).
  • Sustainable economic development.
  • Preservation of biodiversity and ecological balance.
  • An ethical and moral duty towards other living beings.
  • The constitutional mandate under Articles 48A and 51A(g), and Article 21 as judicially expanded.
10Ancient Indian Approach to the Environment

Indian civilisation has traditionally treated nature as sacred – rivers such as the Ganga and Yamuna, mountains, trees such as the peepal and banyan, and animals such as the cow and snake were worshipped or accorded reverence. Vedic literature – hymns of the Rig Veda and Atharva Veda – praises Prithvi (Earth), Varuna (water) and Vayu (air) as deities, emphasising harmony between man and nature through the concept of Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam, ‘the world is one family’.

Kautilya’s Arthashastra prescribed punishments for cutting trees and polluting water bodies, and created state departments for forest management, while Buddhist and Jain philosophy emphasised ahimsa (non-violence) towards all living beings. The Bishnoi community’s historic sacrifice to protect trees later inspired the modern Chipko Movement of the 1970s. This tradition is often invoked by Indian courts – for instance, in cases applying the Public Trust Doctrine – as evidence of an indigenous ethic of environmental stewardship underlying modern environmental jurisprudence.

11Environmental Degradation and Pollution

Environmental degradation is the deterioration of environmental quality through the depletion of resources – soil, water, air, forests – and the destruction of ecosystems, resulting from both natural and anthropogenic causes. Pollution is the introduction of contaminants – physical, chemical or biological agents – into the environment causing adverse change, and is defined under Section 2(c) of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 as the presence of any environmental pollutant.

12Kinds, Causes and Effects of Pollution

Pollution manifests in several distinct forms, each with characteristic causes and effects.

  • Air pollution – caused by vehicular emissions, industrial smoke, burning of fossil fuels and crop-residue burning; leads to respiratory disease, acid rain, smog and global warming.
  • Water pollution – caused by industrial effluents, sewage discharge, agricultural run-off (pesticides/fertilisers) and oil spills; leads to waterborne disease, eutrophication and destruction of aquatic life.
  • Land/soil pollution – caused by industrial waste, pesticides, improper waste disposal and mining; leads to loss of soil fertility and contamination of the food chain.
  • Noise pollution – caused by traffic, industrial machinery, loudspeakers and construction; leads to hearing loss, stress and hypertension.
  • Radioactive pollution – caused by nuclear plants, nuclear testing and improper disposal of radioactive waste; leads to genetic mutation and cancer.
  • Thermal pollution – caused by discharge of heated water from power plants and industries; disrupts aquatic ecosystems.
  • Marine pollution – caused by oil spills, plastic waste and industrial discharge into the seas; destroys marine biodiversity and causes coral bleaching.

Unit-II: Common Law Remedies and Statutory Remedies Against Pollution

1. Trespass 2. Negligence 3. Theories of Strict Liability and Absolute Liability 4. Relevant Provisions of BNS, BNSS and CPC for Abatement of Public Nuisance in Pollution Cases 5. Remedies Under the Specific Relief Act, 1963 6. Reliefs Against Smoke and Noise 7. Noise Pollution
1Trespass

Trespass is a direct and unlawful physical interference with another’s possession of land or property. Where pollutants – smoke, dust, effluents – are directly and physically deposited on another’s land, an action in trespass may lie, since trespass, unlike nuisance, is actionable per se, without proof of damage; the key illustrative case is Gregory v. Piper (1829), where the physical invasion of land by rubbish was held to be trespass. Trespass is distinguished from nuisance by requiring direct interference; where the interference is indirect or consequential, as with drifting smoke, the appropriate action is nuisance rather than trespass.

2Negligence

Negligence is the breach of a legal duty of care resulting in damage to the plaintiff which the defendant ought reasonably to have foreseen, and requires proof of three essential elements – a duty of care owed by the defendant to the plaintiff, breach of that duty, and resulting damage which is not too remote. Industries owe a duty of care to persons and property in the vicinity of their operations, and a failure to take reasonable precautions against the escape of pollutants, such as the leakage of toxic effluents, constitutes negligence. The leading case of Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932) established the modern ‘neighbour principle’ foundational to the law of negligence, which has subsequently been applied in pollution contexts.

3Theories of Strict Liability and Absolute Liability

Under the rule in Rylands v. Fletcher (1868), where a person brings onto his land, for his own purposes, something likely to do mischief if it escapes, and it does escape, he is liable for all damage which is the natural consequence of the escape, even without negligence or fault. The essential elements are a non-natural use of land, the escape of a dangerous thing, and resulting damage; recognised defences under English common law include act of God, act of a stranger, the plaintiff’s own fault, statutory authority, and the plaintiff’s consent. This rule was applied and expanded in India for a time but was found inadequate for hazardous industries because of its numerous exceptions.

In M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (the Oleum Gas Leak case), AIR 1987 SC 1086, arising from the oleum gas leak from Shriram Foods and Fertilizers in Delhi, the Supreme Court, per Bhagwati, C.J., held that the rule in Rylands v. Fletcher, evolved in nineteenth-century England, was not suited to modern industrial society in India, and evolved a new rule of Absolute Liability – where an enterprise is engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity, and harm results to anyone on account of an accident in the operation of that activity, the enterprise is absolutely liable to compensate all those affected, and this liability is not subject to any of the exceptions applicable to the strict liability rule. The measure of compensation must be correlated to the magnitude and capacity of the enterprise so that it acts as a deterrent. This principle was later applied in the aftermath of the Bhopal Gas Tragedy and has become the cornerstone of Indian environmental tort jurisprudence, subsequently also read into statutory schemes such as the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991 and Section 17 (‘no-fault liability’) of the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.

4Relevant Provisions of BNS, BNSS and CPC for Abatement of Public Nuisance in Pollution Cases

Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, corresponding to the former IPC provisions, ‘public nuisance’ is defined, as under the old Section 268 IPC, as an act or illegal omission causing common injury, danger or annoyance to the public or to people in general who dwell in the vicinity. Provisions penalise fouling the water of public springs or reservoirs so as to render it unfit for use (corresponding to former Section 277 IPC), making the atmosphere noxious to health (corresponding to former Section 278 IPC), and negligent conduct with poisonous substances, fire or noxious substances – carried forward in substance under the BNS with renumbering.

Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023, corresponding to the former CrPC, Section 152 BNSS (corresponding to Section 133 CrPC) empowers a District Magistrate, Sub-Divisional Magistrate or Executive Magistrate, on receiving information, to make a conditional order requiring a person to remove a public nuisance – for example, to stop an offensive trade, remove an obstruction, or abate pollution – within a specified time, or to show cause why the order should not be made absolute. This is a speedy, summary remedy widely used in pollution cases, for instance against noise from factories, industrial effluents, or brick kilns. In the landmark case of Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand, AIR 1980 SC 1622, the Supreme Court, per Krishna Iyer, J., held that a Magistrate’s power under Section 133 CrPC to abate public nuisance is a mandatory duty, and that a local authority cannot plead lack of funds as a defence against its statutory obligation to abate a public nuisance – ‘the law will relentlessly be enforced and the plea of poor finance will be poor alibi’.

Under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, Section 91 provides for suits in respect of public nuisance, enabling the Advocate-General, or two or more persons with the leave of the court, to institute a suit for a declaration and injunction or other appropriate relief without proof of special damage, where a public nuisance affects the community; and Order I Rule 8 provides for representative suits, allowing one or more persons to sue on behalf of all persons having the same interest – a provision that was used in public interest environmental litigation before the modern PIL jurisprudence matured. Together these provisions permit civil courts to grant injunctions restraining a polluting activity that constitutes a public nuisance.

5Remedies Under the Specific Relief Act, 1963

Section 38 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 provides for a perpetual injunction to prevent the breach of an obligation, including in cases of continuing nuisance from pollution, such as restraining a factory from discharging effluents; Section 34 provides for declaratory relief, allowing a person to seek a declaration of a legal right where it is denied or threatened, relevant where pollution threatens established rights such as riparian rights; and mandatory injunctions under Section 39, read with Section 38, may be granted to compel a polluter to take positive steps, such as installing an effluent treatment plant, to prevent the continuance of a wrongful act. Injunctions – temporary or interlocutory under Order XXXIX CPC, and perpetual under the Specific Relief Act – are the most commonly sought civil remedy in pollution litigation, since damages alone are often inadequate to stop ongoing environmental harm.

6Reliefs Against Smoke and Noise

Smoke and noise nuisances are actionable both as private nuisance – an unreasonable interference with the use or enjoyment of one’s property – and as public nuisance, where the community at large is affected. The test for private nuisance is the reasonableness of the user, having regard to locality, duration and intensity of the interference: in St. Helen’s Smelting Co. v. Tipping (1865), a distinction was drawn between nuisance causing material or physical damage to property, which is actionable regardless of locality, and nuisance causing mere personal discomfort, where the character of the locality is relevant. Reliefs include prohibitory or mandatory injunctions, damages, and abatement – a self-help remedy of removing the nuisance, exercised with caution and after notice.

7Noise Pollution

Noise pollution is unwanted or excessive sound that disrupts normal activities and adversely affects human health and the environment. It is regulated by the Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000, framed under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, which prescribe ambient noise standards for industrial, commercial, residential and silence zones, restrict the use of loudspeakers, and empower authorities to act against violators.

In Noise Pollution (V), In re, AIR 2005 SC 3136, the Supreme Court held that the right to live in a decent environment, including freedom from noise pollution, is part of the right to life under Article 21, and issued directions regulating the use of loudspeakers and the bursting of firecrackers. Remedies against noise pollution include complaints under the Rules, applications before the SDM/DM under Section 152 BNSS (formerly Section 133 CrPC), civil suits for nuisance, and writ petitions invoking Article 21.

1Law Relating to Preservation of Forests

The Indian Forest Act, 1927 provides for the constitution of reserved, protected and village forests, regulates the movement of forest produce, and prescribes penalties for forest offences. The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 – renamed the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam by a 2023 amendment – restricts de-reservation of forests and the use of forest land for non-forest purposes without prior approval of the Central Government, and was enacted to check large-scale deforestation.

In T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India – the ‘Forest Case’, a continuing mandamus running since 1996 – the Supreme Court gave an expansive dictionary meaning to ‘forest land’, irrespective of ownership or classification, and constituted the Central Empowered Committee (CEC) to monitor forest protection across India.

2Wildlife, Endangered Species, Marine Life, Coastal Ecosystems and Lakes

The Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 provides for the protection of wild animals, birds and plants, creates Schedules I to VI according to the degree of protection, establishes Sanctuaries, National Parks, Conservation Reserves and Community Reserves, regulates or prohibits hunting, regulates trade in animal products, and establishes the National Board for Wildlife and State Boards for Wildlife.

The Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) Notifications, issued under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 – presently the CRZ Notification, 2019 – regulate developmental activities within specified distances of the High Tide Line to protect coastal ecosystems, mangroves and coral reefs, and to safeguard the livelihoods of fishing communities. The Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017 protect lakes, wetlands and water bodies from encroachment, pollution and unsustainable use, complementing India’s obligations under the Ramsar Convention (discussed in Unit V). Marine life is protected under the Wild Life (Protection) Act, the Indian Fisheries Act, 1897, and the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, alongside international conventions such as MARPOL.

3Prevention of Cruelty to Animals

The Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960 penalises cruelty to animals, regulates experimentation on animals, and establishes the Animal Welfare Board of India. It operates alongside Article 51A(g) of the Constitution, which casts a duty on every citizen to have compassion for living creatures, and judicial pronouncements such as Animal Welfare Board of India v. A. Nagaraja (2014), which recognised five freedoms of animals and read Article 21 protections extensively in the context of the Jallikattu litigation.

4Water Pollution – The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974

Enacted pursuant to the Stockholm Declaration, 1972, under Article 252 of the Constitution – by way of resolutions passed by the States enabling Parliament to legislate – the Water Act, 1974 aims at the prevention and control of water pollution, and the maintenance or restoration of the wholesomeness of water.

It establishes the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs); prohibits the discharge of pollutants into streams, wells or sewers beyond prescribed standards without the ‘consent to establish/operate’ of the SPCB under Sections 25/26; empowers the Boards to inspect, sample and take legal action against defaulters; and creates penal provisions under Sections 41–49, including imprisonment and fine for non-compliance and continuing offences. The companion Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Cess Act, 1977 levies a cess on water consumption by industries and local authorities to augment the resources of the Boards and encourage water conservation.

5Air Pollution – The Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981

Also enacted pursuant to the Stockholm Declaration, 1972, the Air Act, 1981 empowers the CPCB and SPCBs – already constituted under the Water Act – to implement air pollution control as well, and to declare Air Pollution Control Areas. It requires the prior consent of the SPCB before establishing or operating any industrial plant in a pollution control area under Section 21, prescribes emission standards, and empowers the Boards to issue directions, including for the closure, prohibition or regulation of industry, and for the stoppage of electricity or water supply under Section 31A. Penal provisions for non-compliance are contained in Sections 37–46.

6Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

Enacted in the aftermath of the Bhopal Gas Tragedy of 1984, the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 is an ‘umbrella legislation’ passed under Article 253 of the Constitution to give effect to decisions taken at the Stockholm Conference, 1972, and to fill gaps in existing environmental laws.

  • Section 3 empowers the Central Government to take all measures necessary to protect and improve the environment, including laying down standards for emission or discharge, restricting industrial location, and managing hazardous substances.
  • Section 5 confers a wide power to issue directions, including for the closure, prohibition or regulation of any industry, and the stoppage or regulation of electricity, water or any other service.
  • Section 7 prohibits the discharge of environmental pollutants in excess of prescribed standards.
  • Sections 8 and 9 impose duties to handle hazardous substances in accordance with procedure and to furnish information of accidents.
  • Section 15 prescribes penalties – imprisonment up to five years or fine up to ₹1 lakh, with additional fine for continuing contravention, or both, with enhanced punishment on repetition.
  • Section 19 restricts cognizance of offences to a complaint by specified authorities, or a sixty-day prior notice by any person to such authority – a much-criticised restriction on citizen-initiated prosecution.

Numerous rules have been framed under the Act, including the Hazardous Waste Rules, the Environment Impact Assessment (EIA) Notification, the CRZ Notification, the Noise Pollution Rules, the Bio-Medical Waste Rules, and the Plastic Waste Management Rules.

7Biological Diversity Act, 2002

Enacted to give effect to India’s obligations under the Convention on Biological Diversity, 1992 (the Rio Earth Summit), the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 aims at the conservation of biological diversity, the sustainable use of its components, and the fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from the use of biological resources and associated traditional knowledge.

Its institutional structure comprises the National Biodiversity Authority (NBA), which regulates access to biological resources by foreign entities and requires approval for obtaining biological resources or knowledge for research, commercial use, or IPR applications; State Biodiversity Boards (SBBs); and Biodiversity Management Committees (BMCs) at the local or panchayat level, which maintain People’s Biodiversity Registers. The Act prohibits the transfer of Indian genetic material outside India without approval, and regulates bio-piracy.

8Hazardous Wastes (Management, Handling and Transboundary Movement) Rules

Framed under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 – presently the Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016, superseding earlier Rules of 1989 and 2008 – these Rules regulate the generation, collection, storage, transport, treatment and disposal of hazardous waste, require authorisation from the SPCBs, and implement India’s obligations under the Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes, 1989, which restricts the import and export of hazardous waste, particularly from developed to developing countries.

9Environmental Pollution Control Mechanism – Institutional Framework

The CPCB and SPCBs carry out standard-setting, monitoring, consent mechanisms and enforcement under the Water Act, Air Act and EPA, while the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) is the apex administrative authority, granting environmental clearances under the EIA Notification, 2006.

Two earlier institutional experiments proved largely ineffective: the National Environment Tribunal Act, 1995, enacted to provide strict liability and speedy relief for damage arising from accidents involving hazardous substances, remained largely un-notified and non-operational; and the National Environment Appellate Authority Act, 1997, which established the NEAA to hear appeals against environmental clearance decisions, functioned slowly with limited benches. Both bodies’ failure to achieve their objectives prompted the enactment of the more robust National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.

10National Green Tribunal – Powers and Jurisdiction

The National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 was enacted for the effective and expeditious disposal of cases relating to environmental protection, the conservation of forests and other natural resources, and for relief and compensation for damage to persons and property, replacing the National Environment Tribunal Act, 1995 and the National Environment Appellate Authority Act, 1997.

Under Section 14, the NGT has jurisdiction over all civil cases involving a substantial question relating to environment – including the enforcement of any legal right relating to environment – arising out of the implementation of the laws specified in Schedule I: the Water Act 1974, the Water Cess Act 1977, the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, the Air Act 1981, the Environment (Protection) Act 1986, the Public Liability Insurance Act 1991, and the Biological Diversity Act 2002.

Under Section 15, the Tribunal may provide relief and compensation to victims of pollution or environmental damage, and order restitution of property and of the environment; under Section 17, liability to pay relief or compensation is imposed on a ‘no-fault’ basis, reflecting the Absolute Liability rule from M.C. Mehta; and under Section 20, the Tribunal is bound to apply the principles of sustainable development, the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle while passing its orders. It possesses civil-court-like powers of summoning, discovery and examination of witnesses, though it is not strictly bound by the CPC or the Evidence Act, being guided instead by principles of natural justice; appeals against its orders lie directly to the Supreme Court under Section 22. The NGT has, in practice, issued landmark directions on the pollution of the Ganga, the Yamuna floodplain, the Art of Living event, and Delhi’s air pollution.

Restriction on Freedom of Trade, Profession and Occupation for the Protection of the Environment 6. Immunity of Environmental Legislation from Judicial Scrutiny – Article 31C 7. Role of Green Belt Development – Purpose and Advantage
1Article 48A of the Constitution

Inserted by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976, under Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy), Article 48A provides that ‘the State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment and to safeguard the forests and wildlife of the country’. Though not directly enforceable in a court of law by virtue of Article 37, it casts a positive obligation on the State, and courts have relied on it to interpret other constitutional provisions, particularly Article 21.

2Article 51A(g) of the Constitution

Also inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, under Part IVA, Article 51A(g) casts a duty on every citizen of India ‘to protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures’. Courts have relied on this provision to impose obligations on citizens and to support directions for environmental protection, for example in cases relating to tree-felling and vehicular pollution.

3Right to a Wholesome Environment

Although not expressly stated in the Constitution, the right to a wholesome environment has been judicially read into Article 21 (the right to life and personal liberty) through a series of landmark decisions.

  • Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra v. State of U.P. (the Dehradun Quarrying Case), AIR 1988 SC 2187 – one of the earliest environmental PILs, in which the Supreme Court ordered the closure of limestone quarries in the Mussoorie hills, treating the right to a healthy environment as part of Article 21.
  • Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar, AIR 1991 SC 420 – held that the right to life under Article 21 includes the right to the enjoyment of pollution-free water and air.
  • M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (the Ganga Pollution, Taj Trapezium and Vehicular Pollution cases) – extended Article 21 to environmental protection across multiple contexts.
  • Virender Gaur v. State of Haryana (1995) – held that Article 21 protects the right to a decent, healthy, pollution-free environment.
4Right to Development

The right to development is recognised as an inherent human right, also affirmed under the UN Declaration on the Right to Development, 1986, and reiterated at the Rio Summit, 1992. Indian courts have consistently sought to balance the right to development with environmental protection through the doctrine of sustainable development – the idea, drawn from the Brundtland Report, ‘Our Common Future’ (1987), that development must meet present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

In Vellore Citizens’ Welfare Forum v. Union of India, AIR 1996 SC 2715, the Supreme Court held that sustainable development, and its essential features – the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle – are part of the environmental law of India, and indeed part of Article 21.

5Restriction on Freedom of Trade, Profession and Occupation for the Protection of the Environment

The right to carry on any trade, business or occupation under Article 19(1)(g) is not absolute, and is subject to reasonable restrictions under Article 19(6) in the interest of the general public. Courts have consistently upheld environmental regulations – closure of polluting industries, relocation orders, denial of licences – as reasonable restrictions in the interest of public health and the environment.

In M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (the Taj Trapezium Case), (1997) 2 SCC 353, the Supreme Court ordered polluting industries around the Taj Mahal to either switch to natural gas or relocate, holding that the right to carry on trade must yield to environmental protection.

6Immunity of Environmental Legislation from Judicial Scrutiny – Article 31C

Article 31C, inserted by the 25th Amendment, 1971 and substantially amended by the 42nd Amendment, 1976 – the extended version being struck down in Minerva Mills v. Union of India, AIR 1980 SC 1789, to the extent it gave blanket protection to all Directive Principles – provides that laws giving effect to the Directive Principles in Article 39(b) and (c) shall not be void merely for being inconsistent with Articles 14 and 19, and shall not be questioned on the ground of taking away those rights.

Insofar as environmental legislation can be shown to give effect to Article 39(b)/(c) – equitable distribution of material resources for the common good – it can invoke this constitutional immunity, though the linkage is not automatic and must be established; and after Minerva Mills, only laws relatable specifically to Article 39(b) and (c), not all Directive Principles, enjoy this immunity.

7Legislative Powers of the Centre and State Government

Under the Seventh Schedule, the Union List contains entries such as the regulation of mines and mineral development (Entry 54) and the regulation of inter-State rivers (Entry 56); the State List contains public health and sanitation (Entry 6), water supplies, irrigation and canals (Entry 17), and fisheries (Entry 21); and the Concurrent List contains forests (Entry 17A) and protection of wild animals and birds (Entry 17B) – both shifted from the State List to the Concurrent List by the 42nd Amendment, 1976.

Article 252 enables Parliament to legislate on a State subject where two or more States consent by resolution, and was the basis for the enactment of the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974; while Article 253 empowers Parliament to legislate for the whole of India to implement international treaties, conventions or decisions, and was the basis for the Air Act, 1981 and the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, both enacted to implement the Stockholm Declaration, 1972.

8Writ Jurisdiction

Article 32 (before the Supreme Court) and Article 226 (before the High Courts) empower courts to issue writs – habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto – for the enforcement of fundamental rights under Article 32, and for any other purpose under the wider scope of Article 226. Environmental jurisprudence in India has developed overwhelmingly through Public Interest Litigation under these Articles, with relaxed rules of locus standi allowing any public-spirited citizen or organisation to approach the court on behalf of affected persons or the environment itself, and innovative remedies such as continuing mandamus, as in the decades-long Godavarman forest case.

9Role of Indian Judiciary in the Evolution of Environmental Jurisprudence

Through a substantial body of PIL-driven case law, the Supreme Court and High Courts have evolved several foundational doctrines of Indian environmental law.

  • Sustainable development – Vellore Citizens’ Welfare Forum (1996).
  • Precautionary principle – anticipatory action to prevent environmental harm even absent full scientific certainty, shifting the burden of proof onto the developer or industrialist to show that an activity is environmentally benign (Vellore Citizens’; A.P. Pollution Control Board v. Prof. M.V. Nayudu, (1999) 2 SCC 718).
  • Polluter pays principle – the polluter is liable for the cost of pollution control, remediation of damage and compensation to victims (Vellore Citizens’; Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India, AIR 1996 SC 1446, the Bichhri village case).
  • Public trust doctrine – natural resources such as air, the sea, waters and forests are held by the State in trust for public use and cannot be transferred to private ownership for commercial use, recognised in M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath, (1997) 1 SCC 388 (the Span Motels case, involving diversion of the Beas river).
  • Absolute liability – M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (Oleum Gas Leak Case), 1987, discussed in Unit II.
  • Inter-generational equity – the present generation holds natural resources in trust for future generations (State of Himachal Pradesh v. Ganesh Wood Products (1995)).

Alongside the expansion of Article 21 to include the right to a healthy environment, the judiciary has increasingly relied on expert committees, continuing mandamus, and monitoring mechanisms to manage complex, polycentric environmental litigation.

10Role of Green Belt Development – Purpose and Advantage

Green belts are zones of planted vegetation or forest cover maintained around industrial areas, cities and highways to act as a buffer against pollution. Their purpose includes the absorption of pollutants and dust, noise attenuation, temperature moderation (mitigating the urban heat-island effect), prevention of soil erosion, aesthetic and recreational value, and the maintenance of ecological balance in rapidly urbanising and industrialising areas.

Their advantages include improved air quality, functioning as a carbon sink, habitat for urban biodiversity, enhanced groundwater recharge, and a visual and physical separation between industrial and residential zones that reduces direct exposure of residential populations to industrial pollution. Green belt development is frequently mandated as a condition of environmental clearance and in municipal or town-planning regulations, and has been directed by courts in specific cases involving polluting industrial clusters.

1International Environmental Regime – Overview

International environmental law comprises treaties, customary international law, general principles, and soft-law declarations governing the conduct of states affecting the global environment, reflecting the reality that environmental harm frequently transcends national boundaries.

2Transnational Pollution and State Liability

Transboundary or transnational pollution is pollution originating in one state that causes harm in another – acid rain, river pollution, nuclear fallout and marine pollution being characteristic examples. The Trail Smelter Arbitration (USA v. Canada), 1941 is the foundational case establishing that no state has the right to use, or permit the use of, its territory in a manner that causes injury to the territory of another state – the origin of the ‘no-harm principle’ (sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas) in international environmental law. States may be held internationally responsible for transboundary environmental harm caused by activities within their territory or control, a principle reflected in Principle 21 of the Stockholm Declaration and Principle 2 of the Rio Declaration.

3Customary International Law

Certain environmental principles are argued to have attained the status of customary international law, binding even on non-signatory states – including the no-harm or good-neighbourliness principle, the duty to conduct environmental impact assessment for activities with significant transboundary effects, and the duty to cooperate and to notify and consult on shared resources. The precautionary principle and the concept of sustainable development, while increasingly influential, remain more contested as elements of custom.

4Liability of Multinational Corporations/Companies

Multinational corporations pose a unique challenge for international environmental law, which traditionally binds states rather than private corporations directly. The Bhopal Gas Tragedy (the Union Carbide disaster of 1984) starkly illustrated the difficulty of holding MNCs accountable across jurisdictions – including questions of forum, since United States courts declined jurisdiction on grounds of forum non conveniens, questions of parent–subsidiary liability, and the perceived inadequacy of compensation under the domestic settlement embodied in the Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, 1985.

Emerging soft-law instruments – the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, 2011 and the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises – increasingly seek to impose environmental and human-rights due-diligence obligations on corporations, though a binding international liability regime for corporate environmental harm remains underdeveloped.

5Stockholm Declaration on the Human Environment, 1972

Adopted at the UN Conference on the Human Environment, Stockholm, June 1972 – the first major international conference to treat environmental issues as a global concern – the Stockholm Declaration contains a Preamble and twenty-six Principles. Principle 1 recognises a fundamental right to freedom, equality and adequate conditions of life in an environment permitting a life of dignity and well-being, while Principle 21 affirms that states have the sovereign right to exploit their own resources, coupled with the responsibility to ensure that activities within their jurisdiction do not cause damage to other states or to areas beyond national jurisdiction. The Declaration led directly to the establishment of UNEP, and inspired India’s own Water Act 1974, Air Act 1981, and Environment (Protection) Act 1986.

6Role of UNEP for the Protection of the Environment

The United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) was established in 1972 pursuant to the Stockholm Conference, and is headquartered in Nairobi, Kenya. Its functions include coordinating international environmental activities, assisting states in developing environmental policy, monitoring the state of the global environment, servicing the secretariats of major environmental treaties – including the CBD, the Basel Convention and the Montreal Protocol – and promoting sustainable development worldwide.

7Ramsar Convention, 1971

The Convention on Wetlands of International Importance, especially as Waterfowl Habitat, was signed at Ramsar, Iran, in 1971. It provides for the designation of wetlands of international importance as ‘Ramsar sites’, and commits parties to promote their conservation and ‘wise use’. India is a party, with a substantial and growing number of Ramsar sites, including Chilika Lake, Keoladeo National Park, and the Sundarbans Wetland.

8Bonn Convention (Migratory Birds), 1992

The Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species of Wild Animals (CMS) was signed at Bonn, Germany, in 1979, and entered into force in 1983, with the aim of conserving terrestrial, aquatic and avian migratory species throughout their range. India acceded to the Convention in 1983, and hosted the CMS COP13 in 2020, at which the ‘Gandhinagar Declaration’ was adopted.

9Nairobi Convention, 1982 (CFCs)

The Nairobi Declaration, 1982 marked the tenth anniversary of the Stockholm Conference, reviewing the state of implementation of the Stockholm Action Plan and expressing concern about ongoing environmental degradation, including concerns over CFCs and ozone depletion, which led subsequently to the Vienna Convention, 1985 and the Montreal Protocol, 1987. A separate, regional ‘Nairobi Convention’ of 1985 addresses protection of the marine and coastal environment of the Eastern African region.

10Biodiversity Convention (Earth Summit), 1992

The Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) was opened for signature at the UN Conference on Environment and Development, the ‘Earth Summit’, held at Rio de Janeiro in 1992. Its three objectives are the conservation of biological diversity, the sustainable use of its components, and the fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from the utilisation of genetic resources – access and benefit-sharing, later elaborated by the Nagoya Protocol, 2010.

The Rio Summit also produced the Rio Declaration on Environment and Development, containing twenty-seven principles including the precautionary and polluter pays principles and the concept of sustainable development, together with the UNFCCC, the UN Convention to Combat Desertification, and Agenda 21, a comprehensive action plan for sustainable development. India enacted the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 to implement its CBD obligations.

11Kyoto Protocol, 1997

Adopted under the UNFCCC at Kyoto, Japan, in 1997, and entering into force in 2005, the Kyoto Protocol set legally binding greenhouse gas emission-reduction targets for developed countries (Annex I parties), based on the principle of ‘common but differentiated responsibilities’, which recognises that developed countries bear greater historical responsibility for GHG emissions. It introduced flexible, market-based mechanisms – the Clean Development Mechanism, Joint Implementation, and Emissions Trading. India, as a developing country, had no binding emission-reduction targets under Kyoto but participated actively in the CDM.

12Johannesburg Convention, 2002

The World Summit on Sustainable Development (WSSD), held at Johannesburg, South Africa, in 2002, took place ten years after the Rio Earth Summit – ‘Rio+10’ – to review progress on Agenda 21. It adopted the Johannesburg Declaration on Sustainable Development and the Johannesburg Plan of Implementation, emphasising poverty eradication, sustainable production and consumption patterns, and the protection of natural resources as pillars of sustainable development.

13UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC)

Adopted at the Rio Earth Summit, 1992 and entering into force in 1994, the UNFCCC has as its objective, under Article 2, the stabilisation of GHG concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that would prevent dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system. It establishes the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities (CBDR-RC), and functions as the parent treaty under which the Conference of Parties (COP) meets annually, and under which both the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement were subsequently adopted. India is a party.

14UN Climate Change Conference (COP21) & Paris Agreement, 2015

COP21 was held in Paris, France, in December 2015, at which the Paris Agreement was adopted; it was opened for signature in 2016 and entered into force in November 2016. The Agreement aims to hold the increase in global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, while pursuing efforts to limit the increase to 1.5°C, and departs from Kyoto’s top-down binding targets in favour of a bottom-up approach, under which each country submits its own Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), to be updated and strengthened every five years through a ‘ratchet mechanism’.

The Agreement retains CBDR-RC, but applies it more flexibly – ‘in light of different national circumstances’ – to all parties, not only developed states, and provides for climate finance, technology transfer and capacity-building support from developed to developing countries, together with a transparency framework for tracking progress on NDCs and a global stocktake every five years. India ratified the Paris Agreement in 2016, and has submitted successive NDCs, including a net-zero emissions target announced at COP26 (Glasgow, 2021) for achievement by 2070.
